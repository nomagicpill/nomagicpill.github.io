Bottlenecks to Goals

Every person, process, and goal has a bottleneck somewhere in the line because not everything can is equal. The pursuit of eliminating bottlenecks essentially just shifts it to a different spot in the line—when capacity in one area outgrows capacity in another, the latter becomes bottlenecked.

Certain things should not be bottlenecks depending on the area; if they are, then measures should be taken to remove it quickly. Time and money are often the bottleneck in growing a startup, as evident by the lack of sleep and (hopefully multiple!) funding rounds the founders go through on their path to glory and riches, both of which are the employees' measures to relieve the time and money constraints, respectively. If they had access to the mythical infinite time and money glitches, then scaling would be easy. But alas, those don't exist, so they're stuck doing things the old-fashioned way.

(This is a blatant message to you, the reader, to take some time to think deeply about your current bottlenecks in life and what you are doing about them, if anything. If you aren't doing anything, then consider why.)

Energy Bottlenecks Are Especially Bad

Lacking energy to do things despite knowing what to do and wanting to want to do it is terrible. The problem is identified, the path clear, but the joules needed just aren't there. For me, there's also a lingering fear that forcing myself to do the thing while motivation and energy are low will breed resentment towards it because of strong negative association.

Hydration

Hydration is generally accepted as something, and even if the evidence is scarce, it's probably worth doing regardless for reasons unrelated to sleep.

Literature:

  • Effects of Fluid Intake on Sleep Duration and Quality Among Healthy Adults:
    Mild dehydration does not appear to affect sleep measures in healthy adults. However, fluid intake correlates positively with REM sleep length, sleep duration, and sleep efficiency, suggesting that the quantity of water consumed may influence sleep quality.
  • Mild Dehydration by 24-h Fluid Restriction Led to Difficulty Falling Sleeping and Increased Sleep Duration:
    Our results indicate mild dehydration increased sleep duration because of increased fatigue and decreased perceived ease of falling asleep. This suggests that maintaining euhydration may assist with perceived ease of falling asleep and feelings of fatigue.

Naps

Naps, when done correctly, can boost alertness and such, but does not replace a full night's sleep.

Avoid naps within 8 hours of bedtime.

Naps should be 30 min or 90 min.

Exercise

Fast(er) Recovery

Quickly recovering from exercise, especially very hard days (100s of TSS, for those who know), is important because a) being absolutely smoked post ride/run/lift/climb is just not fun, despite all the delicious food you can eat without guilt, and b) it may hinder or altogether prevent training sessions in the next few days, resulting in a lower total TSS dose than just going easier in order to exercise the following few days.

Recovering quickly is primarily a function of carbs to replenish glycogen stores, protein to repair muscles, and sleep. None of them can be ignored. Inadequate amounts of any will prolong recovery and decrease overall efficiency.

Training for the New Alpinism claims the 30-to-60-minute post-exercise window is critical for fueling:

    Carbohydrate: A 150-pound climber needs 270–400 calories (70-100 g) of carbohydrate during this post-exercise window. For best recovery repeat this every two hours to re-stimulate glycogen resynthesis for up to six hours.
  • Protein: Consuming protein after a workout is useful to help the body repair damaged muscle, but studies are inconclusive about whether or not the timing is as important as it is for carbohydrate consumption.
  • Fluid. Drink water and replace electrolytes. Adjust your intake to match the temperature and the amount you’ve sweated.
