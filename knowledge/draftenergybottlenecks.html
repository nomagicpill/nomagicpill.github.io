Bottlenecks to Goals

Every person, process, and goal has a bottleneck somewhere in the line because not everything can is equal. The pursuit of eliminating bottlenecks essentially just shifts it to a different spot in the line—when capacity in one area outgrows capacity in another, the latter becomes bottlenecked.

Certain things should not be bottlenecks depending on the area; if they are, then measures should be taken to remove it quickly. Time and money are often the bottleneck in growing a startup, as evident by the lack of sleep and (hopefully multiple!) funding rounds the founders go through on their path to glory and riches, both of which are the employees' measures to relieve the time and money constraints, respectively. If they had access to the mythical infinite time and money glitches, then scaling would be easy. But alas, those don't exist, so they're stuck doing things the old-fashioned way.

(This is a blatant message to you, the reader, to take some time to think deeply about your current bottlenecks in life and what you are doing about them, if anything. If you aren't doing anything, then consider why.)

Energy Bottlenecks Are Especially Bad

Lacking energy to do things despite knowing what to do and wanting to want to do it is terrible. The problem is identified, the path clear, but the joules needed just aren't there. For me, there's also a lingering fear that forcing myself to do the thing while motivation and energy are low will breed resentment towards it because of strong negative association.

Methods to Reduce Energy Bottlenecks

Energy bottlenecks can be reduced by allocating time and money towards certain areas. The same can be said of time bottlenecks (spend money and energy towards things) and money bottlenecks (spend time and energy towards things).

I try to include literature where possible, but have found that literature isn't everything. If something works, it works! Fuck the scientists and "peer-reviewed" journals that claim it doesn't—this is my lived experience. (This is half-joking. Replication is important to an extent, but blindly rejecting an effective treatment because some random study said it doesn't work is not right.)

Some of these overlap with optimizing time because time is energy.

Pre-registration: it's easy to mitigate the floor (i.e., energy improvements by avoiding things that cause lack of energy, e.g., poor sleep, poor lighting, etc.), but difficult to raise the ceiling, at least for extended periods of time. Plenty of quick bumps exist—stimulants, food, cold showers, bright lighting—but they are rarely permanent fixes. The focus should be placed on protecting the downside and strategically supplementing situations. This will result in the total energy over time.

Hydration

Hydration is generally accepted as something, and even if the evidence is scarce, it's probably worth doing regardless for reasons unrelated to sleep.

Literature:

  • Effects of Fluid Intake on Sleep Duration and Quality Among Healthy Adults:
    Mild dehydration does not appear to affect sleep measures in healthy adults. However, fluid intake correlates positively with REM sleep length, sleep duration, and sleep efficiency, suggesting that the quantity of water consumed may influence sleep quality.
  • Mild Dehydration by 24-h Fluid Restriction Led to Difficulty Falling Sleeping and Increased Sleep Duration:
    Our results indicate mild dehydration increased sleep duration because of increased fatigue and decreased perceived ease of falling asleep. This suggests that maintaining euhydration may assist with perceived ease of falling asleep and feelings of fatigue.

On a not-directly-related note, if you have trouble waking up, there are rich-man and poor-man solutions:

  • Rich: Take a delayed-release caffeine pill (example here) before bed to have it hit around your wake-up time. I'd be slightly worried about "microdosing" caffeine throughout the night and that leading to worse sleep quality, but who knows.
  • Poor: Wake up at t minus 30 minutes, take a caffeine pill, then go back to bed until actual wake up time. Caffeine effects start around 15 minutes after taking it, so 30 minutes is plenty and will help waking up.

Exercise

Fast(er) Recovery

Quickly recovering from exercise, especially very hard days (100s of TSS, for those who know), is important because a) being absolutely smoked post ride/run/lift/climb is just not fun, despite all the delicious food you can eat without guilt, and b) it may hinder or altogether prevent training sessions in the next few days, resulting in a lower total TSS dose than just going easier in order to exercise the following few days.

Recovering quickly is primarily a function of carbs to replenish glycogen stores, protein to repair muscles, and sleep. None of them can be ignored. Inadequate amounts of any will prolong recovery and decrease overall efficiency.

Training for the New Alpinism claims the 30-to-60-minute post-exercise window is critical for recovery:

    Carbohydrate: A 150-pound climber needs 270–400 calories (70-100 g) of carbohydrate during this post-exercise window. For best recovery repeat this every two hours to re-stimulate glycogen resynthesis for up to six hours.
  • Protein: Consuming protein after a workout is useful to help the body repair damaged muscle, but studies are inconclusive about whether or not the timing is as important as it is for carbohydrate consumption.
  • Fluid. Drink water and replace electrolytes. Adjust your intake to match the temperature and the amount you’ve sweated.

    CO2 Levels

    Indoor CO2 levels can affect cognitive performance and tiredness levels.

    Solutions are centered around improving ventilation by opening windows or doors, adding fans, or improving recirculation capabilities of the HVAC system. While plants do absorb some CO2, an indoor jungle would be required to appreciably decrease levels. Improving ventilation is the cheapest, easiest, and most effective solution.

    Literature:

    • Is CO2 an Indoor Pollutant? Direct Effects of Low-to-Moderate CO2 Concentrations on Human Decision-Making Performance:
      Relative to 600 ppm, at 1,000 ppm CO2, moderate and statistically significant decrements occurred in six of nine scales of decision-making performance. At 2,500 ppm, large and statistically significant reductions occurred in seven scales of decision-making performance (raw score ratios, 0.06–0.56), but performance on the focused activity scale increased.
    • Associations of Cognitive Function Scores with Carbon Dioxide, Ventilation, and Volatile Organic Compound Exposures in Office Workers: A Controlled Exposure Study of Green and Conventional Office Environments:
      On average, cognitive scores were 61% higher on the Green building day and 101% higher on the two Green+ building days than on the Conventional building day (p < 0.0001). VOCs and CO2 were independently associated with cognitive scores. ["Conventional" had high VOCs, "green" had lows VOCs.]

    Prioritization and Outsourcing

    Sort by Importance and Required Energy

    Eisenhower's important-urgent matrix (or any sorted to-do list, for that matter) could take on a third or replacement of dimension of energy requirement.

    Replacing urgent with energy and prioritizing the four quadrants results in the following rank:

    1. High-energy, high-importance: Ensures there's enough in the tank to complete the activity
    2. Low-energy, high-importance: Ensures the important things get out the door first
    3. High-energy, low-importance and low-energy, low-importance: eliminate or outsource if possible. Shouldn't all activities be somewhat important?
