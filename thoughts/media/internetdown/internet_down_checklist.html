You Are Not Prepared For the Internet Going Down: Checklist

Why each item matters, and the rest of the post: nomagicpill.site/thoughts/draftinternetdown.html. Updated 31 October 2026.

Amounts are per person or per household, for a 30-day plan. For your household's totals, enter it on the page and print your own list.

Practices

First hour

  • Confirm it's really down: restart your phone and router, then ask a neighbor or walk to a store or gas station and see whether their card readers work.
  • Turn on the radio: tune to your area's main AM news station or the NOAA weather channel, and check back on the hour.
  • Charge everything: phones, power banks, laptops, headlamps, and the power station, while the power is still on.
  • Fill the bathtub and any empty containers: water systems run on electric pumps and networked controls, so pressure can drop without warning.
  • Text, don't call: send short texts to family instead of calling, if your phone has any signal.

First day

  • Top up what's still for sale: if a store or gas station is open and taking cash, fill the gas tank and pick up prescriptions and anything you're short on.
  • Check on neighbors: especially older or disabled ones, and anyone who lives alone.
  • Save your phone battery: turn on low power mode, lower the brightness, and switch to airplane mode when you don't need it.
  • Eat from the fridge first: if the power is out too, eat what's in the fridge, then the freezer, then the pantry, and keep the doors shut.
  • Unplug electronics if the power goes out: everything but one lamp, which tells you when it's back.

Until it's back

  • Assume it'll last: plan for at least two weeks until the radio says otherwise.
  • Drive only when you have to: treat dead traffic lights as four-way stops, which is the law in most states.
  • Keep washing your hands: before eating and after using the toilet bucket.
  • Keep a paper log: write down radio updates, which stores and stations are open, and when.

Tier 1

1. Cash

  • Amount: $500-2,000 per household, or a few weeks of normal spending.
  • Small bills: mostly 1s, 5s, 10s, and 20s.

2. Water, 2 weeks

  • Stored water 14 gal per person: 1 gallon per person per day for up to two weeks, in 7-gallon jugs like the Reliance Aqua-Tainer or in cases of bottled water.
  • Filter 1 per household: a squeeze filter like the Sawyer Squeeze (~$40) or a gravity filter like the Platypus GravityWorks.
  • Unscented bleach 1 bottle per household: regular household bleach (5-9% sodium hypochlorite, not scented or "splash-less") as a backup for treating water: 8 drops per gallon of clear water, 16 if it's cloudy, then wait 30 minutes (CDC).
  • Bathtub: fill it the moment you hear about an outage, since water pressure can fail if the utility's pumps or controls go down.

3. Food, 2 weeks of things you already eat

  • Canned goods 14 cans per person: soup, chili, beans, tuna, chicken, vegetables, and fruit.
  • Peanut butter 1 jar per person: about 2,700 calories per 16 oz jar, with protein and fat, no cooking, and months of shelf life.
  • Oats 1 canister per person: rolled or quick oats.
  • Pasta 3 lb per person: cheap calories, but it takes a lot of water and fuel to cook.
  • Rice 3 lb per person: white rice keeps for years; brown rice goes rancid in months.
  • Shelf-stable milk 4 quarts per person: boxed UHT milk (e.g. Horizon or Parmalat) or powdered milk, which keeps for months unopened without a fridge.
  • Ready-to-eat snacks 3 boxes per person: granola or protein bars, crackers, trail mix, and jerky.
  • Coffee or tea, if you drink it: instant coffee works without a coffee maker, and caffeine withdrawal headaches are miserable.
  • Manual can opener 1 per household: a hand-crank one like the OXO Good Grips (~$15).

4. Medicine

  • Spare glasses or contacts: an old pair of glasses or an extra box of contacts.

5. Light, power, and alerts

  • Headlamps 1 per person: e.g. a Petzl Tikka or Black Diamond Spot (~$30-50).
  • AA/AAA batteries 1 pack of 24 per person: enough to replace everything twice.
  • Power banks 1 per person (at least 2): 10,000-20,000 mAh each (e.g. Anker), kept charged.
  • Radio 1 per household: a hand-crank or battery AM/FM/NOAA weather radio, such as the Midland ER310 or Eton FRX3 (~$50-70).
  • Emergency alerts: make sure emergency alerts are turned on in your phone's settings, and sign up for your county's text alerts (Ready.gov explains how).

6. Paper

  • Phone numbers: family, friends, doctors, your landlord, your employer, and an out-of-state contact.
  • Radio frequencies: your area's main AM news station and an FM backup (look up your ZIP code on Radio-Locator), and which of the seven NOAA weather channels covers you (find your county on the National Weather Service's list).
  • Printed local map: a road atlas or printouts, with the places to know marked.
  • Copies of IDs: driver's license, passport, and birth certificate.
  • Insurance info: health insurance cards (front and back), plus your renter's or home and car policy numbers and claims phone numbers.
  • Account numbers: bank, credit cards, and utilities, with their phone numbers, so you can sort things out when systems come back.
  • Medication list: your medications, doses, allergies, and doctors' numbers.

Tier 2

7. Extend food past two weeks

  • White rice 8 lb per person: about 1,650 calories per pound, the cheapest calories that store for years.
  • Dried beans or lentils 5 lb per person: about 1,550 calories per pound, plus the protein and fiber rice lacks.
  • Oats 3 lb per person: about 1,700 calories per pound of breakfast.
  • Peanut butter 2 jars per person: 16 oz jars, about 2,700 calories each.
  • Cooking oil 1 quart per person: about 7,700 calories per quart.
  • Salt 1 canister per household: a 26 oz canister makes rice and beans edible and replaces what you sweat out.
  • Sugar 1 bag (4 lb) per household: quick calories that keep forever.
  • Canned vegetables 6 cans per person: for vitamins and variety.
  • Canned meat or fish 6 cans per person: tuna, chicken, salmon, or Spam.
  • Multivitamin 16 tablets per person: one a day covers what a rice-and-beans diet lacks, such as vitamin C.

8. Internet: Starlink Mini

  • Hardware 1 per household: ~$199, varies by promotion.
  • Standby mode: ~$5-10/month depending on the source, at roughly 0.5 Mbps.
  • Sky view: it needs a clear view of the sky from a balcony or window.

9. Power, minimal (~$1,250)

  • Battery 1 per household: a 1 kWh LiFePO4 power station, e.g. the EcoFlow DELTA 3 Plus.
  • Solar panel 1 per household: one 400 W folding panel, which refills ~1 kWh on a sunny day.

10. Cooking without power

  • Stove 1 per household: a single-burner butane stove (e.g. Gas One or Iwatani, ~$25-90) or a camping canister stove.
  • Fuel 22 canisters per household: 8 oz butane canisters.

11. Sanitation

  • Heavy trash bags 1 box of 20 per person: contractor bags, 3 mil or thicker, in boxes of 20.
  • 5-gallon bucket 1 per household: with a snap-on toilet seat lid, like the Reliance Luggable Loo (~$20-30).
  • Kitty litter or sawdust 1 bag per person: 20-40 lb bags.
  • Toilet paper 9 rolls per person: more than you think.
  • Soap 1 bar per person: bar or liquid.
  • Wipes 3 packs of 80 per person: unscented baby wipes, for washing yourself when you can't shower.
  • Hand sanitizer 1 bottle per person: at least 60% alcohol, for when there's no water to wash with.

12. Warmth and safety

  • Sleeping bag or heavy blankets 1 per person: a sleeping bag rated to 20-30°F for each person, or wool blankets.
  • Fire extinguisher 1 per household: an ABC-rated one, 5 lb or larger (e.g. First Alert or Kidde).
  • CO detector 1 per household: battery-powered, or plug-in with a battery backup, since a plug-in alone dies with the power.
  • Gas tank above half: keep it there from now on.

13. Pets and babies, if applicable

  • Pet food 5 weeks' supply per pet: what they already eat, since a sudden switch upsets their stomachs.
  • Litter 3 bags per cat: the kind your cat already uses.
  • Pet medications, vaccination records, and a carrier or leash 1 per pet: shelters that take pets often require records and crates.
  • Formula 5 weeks' supply per baby: your usual brand.
  • Diapers and wipes 240 diapers per baby: in the current size and the next size up, since babies grow.

14. Morale

  • Paper books 2 books per person: a few you've been meaning to read.
  • Cards and board games 1 per household: a deck of cards, Uno, and a couple of board games.
  • For kids 1 per kid: coloring books, crayons, puzzles, and a favorite small toy.
  • Treats 2 bags per person: chocolate, candy, or whatever your household's comfort food is.
  • Notebooks and pens 1 per household: for the paper log, lists, and notes to neighbors.

Tier 3

15. Offline library

  • Kiwix: a free app for reading downloaded websites offline.
  • Offline maps: Organic Maps or OsmAnd, with your state or region downloaded ahead of time.
  • Printed pages: the 20 that matter: first aid, water purification (the EPA and CDC both have one-page guides), how to shut off your home's water and gas, and a few staple recipes.
  • Local LLM (optional): a model run through Ollama on a laptop; an 8B-parameter model runs on most laptops with 16 GB of RAM.

16. Tools

  • Multitool 1 per household: e.g. a Leatherman or Victorinox.
  • Duct tape 1 roll per household: patches torn tarps, leaky hoses, and broken windows.
  • Work gloves 1 pair per adult: leather, for debris and broken glass.
  • Zip ties 1 pack per household: assorted sizes, for quick fixes.
  • Flashlight 1 per household: a bright handheld one for searching and signaling.
  • Small toolkit 1 per household: screwdrivers, an adjustable wrench, a hammer, and pliers.
  • Small solar panel 1 per household: a 50-100 W folding USB panel keeps phones and power banks charged without the big power station.

17. Manual hand pump or siphon

  • Jiggle siphon 1 per household (~$10-15): moves water from the tub into jugs, or fuel between cans, without sucking on a hose.

18. Power, better (~$5,000)

  • Battery 1 per household: a 4 kWh LiFePO4 power station, e.g. the EcoFlow DELTA Pro 3.
  • Solar 3 panels per household: 400 W folding panels, which together refill ~3 kWh/day in sun.
  • Generator 1 per household: a ~2,500 W dual-fuel inverter generator, which refills the battery in a few hours even in bad weather.
  • Propane 3 tanks per household: 20 lb tanks.

19. Ammunition

  • Ammunition: a few boxes for each firearm you own, stored cool and dry.
  • Secure storage: a lockbox or gun safe, so no one else can get to it.

Full Medicine List

Prescriptions

  • 90-day supply of every daily medication: ask your doctor to write 90-day prescriptions; many insurers cover them, especially by mail order.
  • Backup inhaler, if you use one: keep one at home and one in your bag.
  • Epinephrine auto-injectors, if prescribed: EpiPen or a generic.
  • Insulin and glucose strips, if you use them: plus lancets, pen needles, and glucose tablets for lows.

Pain and fever

  • Acetaminophen 1 bottle per household (Tylenol): fever and pain.
  • Ibuprofen or naproxen 1 bottle per household (Advil, Aleve): pain, fever, and swelling.
  • Low-dose aspirin 1 bottle per household (81 mg): mostly for a suspected heart attack, when chewing four tablets (325 mg) on the way to an ER can help, unless you're allergic or your doctor has told you not to.

Allergy

  • Diphenhydramine 1 box per household (Benadryl): fast relief for hives and allergic reactions.
  • Cetirizine or loratadine 1 box per household (Zyrtec, Claritin): non-drowsy, once a day, for ongoing allergies.
  • Hydrocortisone cream 1 tube per household (1%): rashes, bug bites, poison ivy, and itching.

Stomach and gut

  • Loperamide 1 box per household (Imodium): slows diarrhea so you lose less fluid.
  • Oral rehydration salts 4 packets per person (e.g. DripDrop or WHO-formula ORS packets): replace the salt and water that diarrhea drains, and the most important item in this group.
  • Electrolyte packets 7 packets per person (e.g. Liquid I.V. or LMNT): for heat, sweat, and hard work.
  • Bismuth subsalicylate 1 bottle per household (Pepto-Bismol): upset stomach, nausea, and mild diarrhea.
  • Calcium carbonate antacid 1 bottle per household (Tums): quick heartburn relief.
  • Famotidine 1 box per household (Pepcid): longer-lasting heartburn relief, up to 12 hours.
  • Polyethylene glycol 1 bottle per household (MiraLAX): constipation, which is common when your diet switches to rice and canned food.
  • Meclizine 1 box per household (Bonine, Dramamine Less Drowsy): nausea from motion sickness or vertigo.

Cold and respiratory

  • Saline nasal spray 1 bottle per household (e.g. Simply Saline): congestion, and the dry air that comes with running heaters.
  • Guaifenesin 1 box per household (Mucinex): loosens chest mucus so coughs clear it.
  • Pseudoephedrine 1 box per household (Sudafed, the kind behind the pharmacy counter; bring ID): the decongestant that works.
  • Cough drops 1 bag per household (e.g. Halls or Ricola): sore throat and cough.

Wounds and skin

  • Antibiotic ointment 1 tube per household (Polysporin or Neosporin): keeps minor wounds moist and clean.
  • Sterile gauze 1 box per household: 4x4 pads and a roll or two, for covering wounds and pressing on bleeding.
  • Adhesive bandages 1 box per household: assorted sizes.
  • Medical tape 1 roll per household: paper or cloth, to hold gauze in place.
  • Elastic wrap 1 per household (ACE bandage): sprains, and holding dressings on.
  • Saline or clean water 1 per household, with an irrigation syringe or squeeze bottle: enough pressure to flush dirt out of a wound.
  • Povidone-iodine 1 bottle per household (Betadine): kills germs on the skin around a wound.
  • Burn gel 1 per household (e.g. Water-Jel): for minor burns, after 20 minutes under cool running water.
  • Tweezers 1 per household: fine-point, for splinters, glass, and ticks.
  • Trauma shears 1 per household: cut clothing away from an injury without cutting the person.
  • Tourniquet 1 per household: a CAT or SOF-T, bought from a reputable seller since counterfeits are common.

Eyes and mouth

  • Lubricating eye drops 1 bottle per household (e.g. Systane or Refresh): dry eyes from heat, smoke, or wearing contacts too long.
  • Eyewash 1 bottle per household: sterile saline, for flushing out dust or chemicals.
  • Contact solution: a full bottle and a spare case.
  • Temporary dental filling kit 1 per household (e.g. Dentemp) and clove oil: the kit covers a lost filling or cap, and clove oil numbs tooth pain.

Monitoring

  • Digital thermometer 1 per household: tells a mild illness from a fever that needs care.
  • Pulse oximeter 1 per household (fingertip, ~$20-30): measures blood oxygen.
  • Blood pressure cuff 1 per household: an automatic upper-arm cuff (e.g. Omron); wrist cuffs are less accurate.
  • Glucose meter, if diabetic: plus spare strips and lancets.

Hygiene and protection

  • Nitrile gloves 1 box of 100 per household: they protect you and the wound during first aid, and your hands when emptying the toilet bucket.
  • N95 masks 5 masks per person: NIOSH-approved, for smoke, dust, mold cleanup, and illness.
  • Insect repellent 1 bottle per household: 20-30% DEET or picaridin, for mosquitoes and ticks.
  • Sunscreen 1 bottle per household: SPF 30 or higher.
  • Menstrual products: tampons, pads, or a menstrual cup, plus Midol or ibuprofen for cramps.

Places to Know Nearby

  • Hospital emergency room (find nearest): if phones are down, you can't call 911, so the ER is where you go instead.
  • Fire and police stations (find nearest fire or police): they're staffed around the clock and talk over their own radio systems, which don't depend on the internet or cell towers.
  • Pharmacy (find nearest): prescriptions are the one thing you can't improvise.
  • Grocery stores (find nearest): know two or three, including one within walking distance.
  • Gas stations (find nearest): pumps need power and card readers need a network.
  • Bank or credit union branch (find nearest): ATMs and cards will likely be down, and a branch might still hand out cash against your ID if it opens at all.
  • Shelters, warming centers, and cooling centers: usually schools, libraries, community centers, and churches your county designates ahead of time.
  • Hardware store (find nearest): batteries, propane, tarps, water containers, and generators sell out first.
  • Nearest fresh water (find nearest): a creek, lake, or pond is what your filter is for if the taps stop.
  • Your family's meeting place: see For Your Family.

Upkeep

  • Water: refill containers you filled from the tap, and replace store-bought bottles past their date.
  • Food: eat and replace anything near its date, oldest first.
  • Batteries and power banks: test the headlamps, flashlight, and radio, and recharge the power banks and power station.
  • Medicine: replace anything expired, especially epinephrine, insulin, and liquids.
  • Paper: update phone numbers, your medication list, and the places on your map.
  • Safety gear: test the CO detector, and check that the fire extinguisher's gauge is in the green.
  • Starlink, if you have it: power it on and make sure Standby mode still connects.
  • Family plan: make sure everyone still knows the out-of-state contact and the meeting place.
  • Bleach: replace it once a year, since it loses strength.

For Your Family

  • A shared plan: one out-of-state contact everyone calls or texts, and a physical meeting place if phones are down.
  • A shared checklist: a spreadsheet or printed page showing who has what, so you're not all buying the same things.
