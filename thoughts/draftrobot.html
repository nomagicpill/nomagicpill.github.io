A Job of Procedures and Checklists on the Path to Efficiency

My job as an engineer in a semiconductor fab comes with a lot of procedures and checklists. Procedures and checklists are great because they both teach and remind people the proper way to do things, ensuring the task is done the same way—the best, safest, most efficient, properly-ordered way—every time. This strategy minimizes variation in the task, in turn minimizing the amount of troubleshooting, redos, and issues later on. Great!

My job also comes with a lot of tasks. My to-do list, when broken down into individual subtasks, is well over 500 items. Because of this, it's incredibly important that I'm efficient with my time and energy throughout the workday. This may take the form of delegation, prioritization, and, you guessed it, procedures.

Everything has become procedurized at work. Making a change to a document? Let me walk through my change-a-document procedure and checklist. Updating a tool setting? I need to follow my change-a-tool-setting document. This actually works very well—I miss fewer things, and things I do miss get added so they aren't missed again. The procedures only get longer, never shorter; they are always followed, never skipped. It also outsources my "hmm, what exactly do I need to do and check again?" thinking process to a numbered list on my computer, freeing up valuable brain space for actual processing. All of this increases my efficiency at work. I can see the number of tasks I do go up over time and the number of do-it-agains go down over time. (The number of tasks going up isn't Goodharting as these actually need to be done and I'm not making them up.) I get told I'm a workhorse and doing a good job at it. I look at my procedures and beam with pride at my panacea.

Bleeding Into My Personal Life

The problem is that my procedure obsession has bled into my personal life.

A few examples of procedures that I've implemented (and think are great) that help me get things done more quickly and without as much mental effort:

  • My nightly routine where I get wind down the current day and get ready for the following day
  • My partner and I's weekly relationship check-in
  • My get-home-from-work checklist
  • My partner and I's words for having clear communication

But as a result I've become less capable of operating without a procedure. Like a dumb robot that's programmed to only do a few things and struggles with a novel request. I resort to procedurization any time I feel inefficient with something or want to improve its quality, further eroding my ability to free form on a regular basis. What has this cost me? Do I have a worse working memory because I write everything down instead of keeping it up in my head?

Becoming More Like Your Job

I have this pet theory that people often change to become more like what their job demands of them. It's hard to not become more like something that takes up a third of your life every day!

Take human resources (HR). They often see things in black and white: was a rule broken or not? If yes, do X. If not, do Y. They can struggle to see things in shades of grey and with the necessary context, where one situation is fine and one is not despite being identical. Does this transfer to their personal lives? If their partner does something "wrong", do they consider all the information before deciding it's wrong, or do they simply plug it into their human_resources_mind program and check the output?
