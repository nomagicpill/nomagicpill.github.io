A Job of Procedures and Checklists on the Path to Efficiency

My job a semiconductor fab engineer comes with a lot of procedures and checklists. They're great because they both teach and remind people the proper way to do things, ensuring the task is done the same way, the best way, the safest way, the most efficient way, and the properly-ordered way every time. This strategy minimizes variation in the task, in turn minimizing the amount of troubleshooting, redos, and issues later on. Awesome!

My job also comes with a lot of tasks. My to-do list is well over 500 not-small items when including projects broken down into individual subtasks. Because of this, it's incredibly important that I'm efficient with my time and energy throughout the workday. This may take the form of delegation, prioritization, and, you guessed it, procedures that allow me to just do and not think.

Everything has become procedurized at work. Making a change to a document? Let me walk through my change-a-document procedure and checklist. Updating a tool setting? I need to follow my change-a-tool-setting procedure. This actually works very well—I miss fewer things, and things I do miss get added so they aren't missed again. The procedures only get longer, never shorter; they are always followed, never skipped. It also outsources my "hmm, what exactly do I need to do and check again?" thinking process to a numbered list on my computer, freeing up valuable brain space for actual thinking. All of this increases my efficiency at work. I can see the number of tasks I do go up over time and the number of do-it-agains go down over time. (The number of tasks going up isn't Goodharting as these actually need to be done and I'm not making them up.) I get told I'm a workhorse and doing a good job at it. I look at my procedures and beam with pride. My equipment performance improves and makes my life easier. I become hooked on the positive reinforcement and keep pushing.

Wait a second... if this works so well at work, maybe it can work just as well at home!

Bleeding Into My Personal Life

Wrong. Well, kind of. My procedure obsession has bled into my personal life, but whether it's made my life worse or not is up for debate.

A few examples of procedures (see Appendix I for details) that I've implemented and think are great that help me get things done more quickly and without as much mental effort:

  • My nightly routine where I get wind down the current day and get ready for the following day
  • My partner and I's weekly relationship check-in
  • My get-home-from-work (mental) checklist
  • My partner and I's words for having clear communication

But even if all of these have helped certain aspects of my life, I've become less capable of operating without a procedure as a result. Like a dumb robot that's programmed to only do a few things super well and struggles with a novel request. I resort to procedurization any time I feel inefficient with something or want to improve its quality, further eroding my ability to free form on a regular basis. What has this cost me? Do I have a worse working memory because I write everything down instead of keeping it up in my head? It sure seems like I forget a lot if I don't immediately write it down. It's certainly cost me the spontaneity that I used to have; I now have rigid schedules when I get home, a slave to Focusmates and my ever-growing to-do list of stuff I gotta do. It's cost me flexibility; I now have to know roughly when and where things are happening on trips, during the day, and so on, lest we miss something or do something wrong or who knows maybe I'm just making up boogeymen in my mind.

Becoming More Like Your Job

I have this pet theory that people often change to become more like what their job demands of them. It's hard to not become more like something that takes up a third of your life every day! It's also probably more beneficial to change towards the job personality so there's no having to choose between two personas and there's an extra eight hours of practice a day. (There's also the explanation that jobs select for certain types of people and vice versa, so it's some self-reinforcing feedback loop.)

Take human resources (HR). They can often see things in black and white: was a rule broken or not? If yes, do X. If not, do Y. Seeing things in shades of grey and with the necessary context can be a struggle. Does this transfer to their personal lives? If their partner does something "wrong", do they consider all the information before deciding it's wrong, or do they simply plug it into their human_resources_mind program and check the output? While I doubt it's that B&W, I suspect they're more likely to behave in the B&W manner than they were before they started working there.

Relationship Check-in

My partner and I discuss the following (written by Claude based on input from me):

  1. Roses and thorns: Each person shares one high and one low from the week. No problem-solving, just listening. Sets the tone and makes sure you're both actually caught up on each other's lives.
  2. Anything on your mind: Open floor — grievances, things you've been meaning to bring up, appreciations, whatever. This is the pressure valve. Having a scheduled slot for this prevents things from festering.
  3. Plan the week: Look at both calendars together. Schedule the two cooking nights, the date night, and any logistics (who's doing what chores, any commitments coming up). Concrete and brief.
  4. Relationship tasks: A short standing checklist — bills, any upcoming events to plan for, family obligations, whatever recurs for you two. Keep an actual shared list so you're not trying to remember it mid-meeting.

Get-Home-From-Work Checklist

As soon as I walk in the door I:

  1. Take off my shoes
  2. Place my phone in the phone safe
  3. Wash my lunch container and utensils
  4. Change clothes
