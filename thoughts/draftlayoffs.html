Indiscriminate Layoffs Lead to Firm Inefficiency

Published

Low trust, low morale, low incentives.

Contents

Background

The company I work for recently laid off 200 of my coworkers at another site (call it site L for layoffs). The official reason is to help the long-term strategy of the company. The unofficial reason is that they were bloated relative to other sites (i.e., site L had three employees for every one site X had), weren't turning a profit, and weren't making progress as fast as executives would have liked.

All of my good, competent counterparts are gone; people with 10-20+ years of experience are now out the door, leaving behind one incompetent person and one competent, yet inexperienced, person. They will now each be doing the work of three people. The same is true of other groups; the valuable employees (read: high salary) have been given the boot,

Layoff Theory

Layoffs happen because of costs. Period. The secondary reason varies:

  • Trying to get rid of higher paid employees in hopes of hiring lower paid employees (lower costs)
  • Trying to get rid of any employees because they are not needed at that time (lower costs)
  • Trying to find better employees to replace the current ones (higher productivity)

The preciseness and amount of calculation that goes into deciding who gets laid off is then chosen accordingly to match the firm's goals. Cost-cutting to try and reach short-term goals to satisfy investors? Use a shotgun to cut the top earners and worry about the long-term consequences later. Pivoting to a different business line, but still need technical talent? Use a scalpel to cull the poor performers while retaining top talent. Need a combination of both? Choose a bit from both the top and bottom.

Impact of Layoffs on Employees

Literature

Summarized by Claude and checked by me, Brockner et al.'s Layoffs, Equity Theory, and Work Performance: Further Evidence of the Impact of Survivor Guilt found that employees worked harder when layoffs appeared random because they wanted to compensate for survivor's guilt, whereas employees didn't change performance when layoffs were merit-based because they convinced themselves of the quality of their work.

Claude's summary:

Key Findings

  1. Participants in both layoff conditions reported feelings of "positive inequity" (being treated more fairly than the person laid off)
  2. Both layoff groups reported higher levels of guilt compared to the control group
  3. Those in the random layoff condition showed the greatest increase in work quantity between tasks
  4. Those in the merit-based layoff condition showed the least decline in work quality
  5. Merit condition participants psychologically justified their survivor status by rating their first-task performance more highly

Theoretical Explanations

The results strongly support equity theory, which proposes that:

  • Perceived inequity leads to emotional discomfort (guilt)
  • People are motivated to reduce this discomfort through behavioral or psychological means
  • Random survivors reduced guilt by working harder (behavioral compensation)
  • Merit-based survivors reduced guilt by focusing on their superior performance (psychological justification)
