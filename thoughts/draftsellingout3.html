You Are Not Immune to Feedback

It's difficult to receive external feedback and not change direction in some form. The desire to impress others, to make money, to gain status, to feel validated, to see numbers go up is incredibly strong, enough to convince us of or help justify things we didn't previously believe, thought were evil, etc. What may have started as a hobby in a creative art has turned into an exercise in figuring out how to satisfy some external goal—money, status, validation—as dictated by the audience. The next project, one that previously would have brought excitement and interest, is now being tweaked to get just a bit more exposure, a bit more agreement from the audience, a bit more revenue, in a way that it wouldn't have been before.

Depending on the community that the content is being posted in, a lot of feedback comes from average people, making it average feedback and unhelpful towards improving. The exceptional, prolific creators are out there in the world doing their own thing, rarely taking the time to provide feedback to the young'uns and causing feedback to be overindexed on the people whose opinions aren't really that valuable, but still feels good because, well, getting praise feels good!

Listening to the average audience is bad for the creative because it either a) lulls them into a false sense of security that their work is good, or, worse yet, b) changes their output from their true self and into something that maximizes engagement for said average audience. Weren't they doing it for themselves at the beginning?

"But the Market Is Telling Me Something!"

The market is always telling you something! The question whether it's a market worth listening to. Most hobbyist creatives aren't doing what they do to satisfy some niche market opening—they're doing it for themselves and because they like it; a pursuit that incurs a cost with no expectation of reward at the end is driven by love for the game. (This isn't to say that pursuing something for money isn't done for love for the game, but the love is different.)

Markets corrupt and change behaviors as people respond to feedback and incentives in an effort to gain resources, often coming at the expense of the seller's original creative vision and intention. They sell out in an effort to satisfy their fans, both new and old, perverting their soul in the process without realizing it.

Alternatives

So what's the alternative? Going full hermit mode in a cabin in Montana doesn't offer a chance to test ideas in the arena of society (unless you write a manifesto), an important part of getting better at anything. Companies can't build, test, and sell a product in secret if they want to make a better product: they need feedback in the form of sales, lack of sales, and comments from consumers. The same principle applies to the creative arts. Writing, for example, should be put out into the world so others can read it and either tear it to shreds or go on Substack and call it the best thing they've ever read to show they have good taste, but without offering anything else of substance to avoid proving they have analytical commentary skills. The quality holes can then slowly get patched and certain styles reinforc—wait a sec! Was that style really you? Weren't you just testing that phrase to see what happened? You didn't think it would actually take off, did you? That microfame thing is actually pretty cool, right? Next thing you know, you'll probably have somebody in the blogosphere recognize your name and writing and actually praise you for it!

The key to the feedback here is weighting and filtering. Filter out or discount all the buffoons, the charlatans, the low-effort anons, the people whose opinions aren't that valuable. Focus on the best comments and people of the creative world you inhabit and work to gain their attention and, ultimately, respect. One shoutout from a Scott Alexander is worth 100 shoutouts from the GenericUsername123s of the world. These creators' time and attention are precious, meaning if they choose to read your stuff and comment on it, it must be pretty good.

It will be at that moment that you know you made it.
