Hold Money Hostage to Get Things Done

Published 2026-11-20

Why financial commitment devices are the best thing ever. No money was harmed or lost in the writing of this post.

Contents

Using Willpower is Unwise

Do you really think that using willpower is a wise choice? That it's like a muscle you can grow? That the daily/weekly/monthly/yearly/lifetime reservoir won't run dry eventually, leaving you to drag your feet and rely on caffeine and Adderall to carry you to the finish line? That there aren't better things you could apply it to? That there aren't more efficient, easier methods out there of getting things done? That it makes you look cooler or stronger or better? That it somehow makes your life better?

I used to believe these things, using brute force on everything and thinking that hard work was the universal solvent, the panacea for all my goals and woes. But the willpower well is finite and should be used sparingly lest it empties before the point where it's really needed.

Money as a Fire Under Your Ass

Money is the ultimate commitment device:

  • The likelihood that the task gets done scales with the amount of money on the line. Staking $5 to do something increases the chances by a lot; staking $500 to do something increases the chances enough to effectively guarantee it gets done barring some emergency or disaster.
  • The amount of money to stake is effectively infinite (up to net worth). It can be cents, dollars, or some not-small percentage of one's net worth. Decide how important or dreadful/inconvenient/ugh-y the task is to get done, then decide the appropriate dollar amount. A task that will definitely get done, like eating, is $0; a task that is a massive pain gets a lot more.
  • The types of contracts and amount of them one can enter is infinite. The typical Beeminder derail penalty increments go $5 → $10 → $30 → $90 → $270 (→ $810 → $2430 → $7290 are optional). Want to make it steeper instead and have the commitment be $5n, where n is the number of failures? Go for it!
  • Proof can be required to eliminate lying or weaseling out of it. Get a friend or family member to require proof.

And yet most people, even the ones who know about them, don't use them enough or at all. Why?

(Poor) Reasons for Not Using FCDs

It can feel weird to say "I'll give up $20 if I don't call my mom by Saturday" because it implies the phone call is worth $20, and if the bettor doesn't call, then she definitely isn't worth $20—sorry, Mom! That feels especially shitty, so avoiding the commitment avoids the chance of feeling crappy about a preference being revealed altogether.

Or it makes the person feel weak, like they have to threaten themselves in order to do something, regardless of whether or not they actually want to do it. I often want to call my mom and know it's the right thing to do, but my life can get busy and to-do list long, so staking some money on it ensures I prioritize it the appropriate amount; otherwise it may fall by the wayside via excuses or forgetfulness or exhaustion.

Or the thing that they claim is so important to them (and thus would be a perfect candidate for a well-written contract) isn't actually as important as they thought upon further consideration, so they avoid committing anything. Revealing preferences to oneself and others can be scary. But isn't there something, anything, that's important enough to put money on to help ensure it's going to get done? If you're so sure you're going to do it and want to do it and need to do it, what's the harm in putting $20 on the line?

Or they're afraid that some emergency will come up and they won't be able to fulfill their action. Then just have it be far enough out and with low enough stakes that it effectively minimizes the risk of an emergency completely derailing the plan. If something comes up and stops a person in the tracks for a week or more, there's more to worry about than the measly money that was put on the line!

Or the stakes are so high that they cause anxiety and they just don't want to think about it because of how badly it'll hurt if they lose so they just avoid committing to anything altogether to avoid potential pain. The solution is simple: lower the stake! Or make the commitment more achievable (but not trivial)! These are analog values that can be adjusted and fine-tuned as much as needed. Use the knob advantageously.

Or the commitment is dependent on others and it's not their money that's on the line! But why structure it in a way that's that dependent on others? What about just, you know, talking to them about it and explaining that it's important and to please help?

While these are all understandable reasons, they are still blockers to the goal being achieved more reliably and efficiently and should be resolved.

Is There Evidence for FCDs Actually Working?

Yes, but it's a bit tainted because most people don't sign up. Maybe this is telling?

Here are some supportive studies with results I cherry-picked:

  • Giné et al.'s Put Your Money Where Your Butt Is: A Commitment Contract for Smoking Cessation:
    Of smokers offered CARES [the study's FCD], 11 percent took up, and smokers randomly offered CARES were 3 percentage points more likely to pass the 6-month test than the control group. More importantly, this effect persisted in surprise tests at 12 months, indicating that CARES produced lasting smoking cessation.
  • Halpern et al.'s Randomized Trial of Four Financial-Incentive Programs for Smoking Cessation:
    Reward-based programs were associated with higher abstinence rates than deposit-based programs (15.7% vs. 10.2%, P<0.001). However, in instrumental-variable analyses that accounted for differential acceptance, the rate of abstinence at 6 months was 13.2 percentage points (95% confidence interval, 3.1 to 22.8) higher in the deposit-based programs than in the reward-based programs among the estimated 13.7% of the participants who would accept participation in either type of program.
  • Volpp et al.'s Financial Incentive–Based Approaches for Weight Loss: A Randomized Trial:
    The incentive groups lost significantly more weight than the control group (mean, 3.9 lb). ... The use of economic incentives produced significant weight loss during the 16 weeks of intervention that was not fully sustained.
  • Sykes-Muskett et al.'s The Utility of Monetary Contingency Contracts for Weight Loss: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis:
    There was a significant small-to-medium effect of MCCs on weight loss during treatment when one outlier study was removed. Group refunds, deposit not paid as lump sum, participants setting their own deposit size and additional behaviour change techniques were associated with greater weight loss during treatment.
  • Royer et al.'s Incentives, Commitments, and Habit Formation in Exercise: Evidence from a Field Experiment with Workers at a Fortune-500 Company:
    workers responded strongly to an incentive targeting use of the company gym, but long‐run effects were modest, at best. However, workers in the treatment arm that combined the incentive program with a commitment contract option showed long‐lasting behavioral changes, persisting even 1 year after the incentive ended.
  • Kaur et al.'s Self-Control at Work:
    workers increase output by voluntarily choosing dominated contracts (which penalize low output but give no additional rewards for high output)
  • Ashraf et al.'s Tying Odysseus to the Mast: Evidence from a Commitment Savings Product in the Philippines:
    After twelve months, average savings balances increased by 81 percentage points for those clients assigned to the treatment group [who were offered an account that restricted access to their savings] relative to those assigned to the control group.
  • John's When Commitment Fails – Evidence from a Field Experiment found people with self-control issues had problems with a certain commitment device and gave the possible explanation that:
    the chosen penalties were too low (the commitment was too weak) to overcome clients’ self-control problems. Both take-up and default are negatively predicted by measures of sophisticated hyperbolic discounting - suggesting that partial sophisticates adopt weak commitments and then default, while full sophisticates are more cautious about committing, but better able to choose incentive-compatible contracts.

What to take away from the literature?

First, stake amounts matter. Stake enough money that it's painful to lose it. This amount can be determined by slowly working up to a personal pain point or just straight jumping into two- or three-figure numbers. While not necessarily studied, going too high too quickly may discourage FCD use because of too much pain, so erring on the side of caution at first and slowly working up is probably better.

Second, the contract(s) should stay on. Beeminder does this by enforcing user-defined periodic checks, while Spondeo and TaskRatchet are for one-off tasks. Both serve their purpose.

Lying, A Common Objection to FCDs

Automatic, can't-tamper-with-or-fake verification is important, with the honor system a close second. As Beeminder/dreev aptly puts it when asked about whether you can just lie about data:

Well, if you were the type who would falsify your data to weasel out of paying what you pledged on your Beeminder goal, then you probably would've rolled your eyes and walked away a long time ago, certainly before making it to the depths of our help pages. So you could, but you're not going to. Right?

Right! But tamperproof verification is still helpful to keep people honest from lying, fibbing, or mental gymnasticing. So how do you make this happen?

Beeminder has both built-in data integrations and the ability to make custom ones so that it's more difficult to fake data. Spondeo requires a picture that's reviewed by a human (for now). Non-app commitment can have a trusted referee hold the money until the commitment is proven one way or the other.

The honor system and being that type of person is still underrated here. Being the type of person to sign up for something like this and intentionally lying about the commitment just don't mix. Guilt will be felt.

Methods

Here's a list of financial commitment apps:

  • Beeminder: Used for building habits. They are constantly adding new features.
  • Spondeo: Used for one-off tasks. Requires proof of completion.
  • TaskRatchet: Used for one-off tasks. Does not require proof of completion.

You can also just make commitments offline and do many things with the forfeited money, such as destroy it, donate it to a good cause, or donate it to a bad cause.
