Using Willpower is Unwise

Do you really think that using willpower is a wise choice? That it's like a muscle you can grow? That the daily/weekly/monthly/yearly/lifetime reservoir won't run dry eventually, leaving you to drag your feet and rely on caffeine and Adderall to carry you to the finish line? That there aren't better things you could apply it to? That there aren't more efficient, easier methods out there of getting things done? That it makes you look cooler or stronger or better? That it somehow makes your life better?

I used to believe these things, using brute force on everything and thinking that hard work was the universal solvent, the panacea for all my goals and woes. But the willpower well is finite and should be used sparingly lest it empties before the point where it's really needed.

Money as a Fire Under Your Ass

Money is the ultimate commitment device:

  • The likelihood that the task gets done scales with the amount of money on the line. Staking $5 to do something increases the chances by a lot; staking $500 to do something increases the chances enough to effectively guarantee it gets done barring some emergency or disaster.
  • The amount of money to stake is effectively infinite (up to net worth). It can be cents, dollars, or some not-small percentage of one's net worth. Decide how important or dreadful/inconvenient/ugh-y the task is to get done, then decide the appropriate dollar amount. A task that will defintiely get done, like eating, is $0; a task that is a massive pain gets a lot more.
  • The types of contracts and amount of them one can enter is infinite. The typical Beeminder derail penalty increments go $5 → $10 → $30 → $90 → $270 (→ $810 → $2430 → $7290 are optional). Want to make it exponential instead and have the commitment be $5n, where n is the number of failures? Go for it!
  • Proof can be required to eliminate lying or weaseling out of it. Get a friend or family member to require proof.

And yet most people, even the ones who know about them, don't use them enough or at all. Why?

(Poor) Reasons for Not Using FCDs

It can feel weird to say "I'll give up $20 if I don't call my mom by Saturday" because it implies the phone call is worth $20, and if the bettor doesn't call, then she definitely isn't worth $20—sorry, Mom! That feels especially shitty, so avoiding the commitment avoids the chance of feeling crappy about a preference being revealed altogether.

Or it makes the person feel weak, like they have to threaten themselves in order to do something, regardless of whether or not they actually want to do it. I often want to call my mom and know it's the right thing to do, but my life can get busy and to-do list long, so staking some money on it ensures I prioritize it the appropriate amount; otherwise it may fall by the wayside via excuses or forgetfulness or exhaustion.

Or the thing that they claim is so important to them (and thus would be a perfect candidate for a well-written contract) isn't actually as important as they thought upon further consideration, so they avoid committing anything. Revealing preferences to oneself and others can be scary. But isn't there something, anything, that's important enough to put money on to help ensure it's going to get done? If you're so sure you're going to do it and want to do it and need to do it, what's the harm in putting $20 on the line?

Or they're afraid that some emergency will come up and they won't be able to fulfill their action.

Or the stakes are so high that they cause anxiety and they just don't want to think about it because of how badly it'll hurt if they lose so they just avoid committing to anything altogether to avoid potential pain. The solution is simple: lower the stake! Make the commitment more achievable (but not trivial)!

Or the commitment isn't dependent on others and it's not their money that's on the line!

While these are all understandable reasons, they are still blockers to the goal being achieved more reliably and efficiently and should be resolved.

Is There Evidence for FCDs Actually Working?

Lying, A Common Objection to FCDs

Automatic, can't-tamper-with-or-fake verification is important, with the honor system a close second. As Beeminder/dreev aptly puts it when asked about whether you can just lie about data:

Well, if you were the type who would falsify your data to weasel out of paying what you pledged on your Beeminder goal, then you probably would've rolled your eyes and walked away a long time ago, certainly before making it to the depths of our help pages. So you could, but you're not going to. Right?

Right! But tamperproof verification is still helpful to keep people honest from lying, fibbing, or mental gymnasticing. So how do you make this happen?

Again, the honor system and being that type of person do a lot of work here. Don't you feel guilty lying like that? Don't you know that marking that commitment—the one that didn't actually get done—as complete is wrong? The solution is a) accountability in the form of a person, and b) being a grown-up and owning up to shortcomings. Seriously. A commitment was made. Now honor it.

Methods

Here's a list of financial commitment apps:

  • Beeminder: Used for building habits. They are constantly adding new features.
  • Spondeo: Used for one-off tasks. Requires proof of completion.
  • TaskRatchet: Used for one-off tasks. Does not require proof of completion.

You can also just make commitments offline and do many things with the forfeited money, such as destroy it, donate it to a good cause, or donate it to a bad cause.
