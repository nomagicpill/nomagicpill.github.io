Start Here

If you only do five things this week (about $150-300, plus the cash itself):

  1. Water: 1 gallon per person per day for two weeks, so 14 gallons each.
  2. Food: two weeks of what you already eat, plus a manual can opener.
  3. Cash: $500-2,000 in small bills.
  4. Radio and light: a hand-crank or battery radio, a headlamp for each person, and spare batteries.
  5. Paper and prescriptions: write down phone numbers, your medications, and the nearest hospital and pharmacy, and ask your doctor about 90-day refills.

What It Would Be Like

You wake up and roll over for your phone to check last night's situational happenings on the Twitter timeline. It isn't loading. You check another app and that isn't loading either. It's then that you notice the "SOS" on the top right. No three Wi-Fi bars, no LTE, no 5G. Just "SOS". Hmm, that's weird. You restart your phone, but no dice—those three pesky letters are staring right back, taunting and standing in the way of you and your informative morning routine.

The same is true for the laptop and the television. You knock on your neighbor's door and ask if you can borrow their phone to check on your girlfriend, but theirs is also out. The knot in your stomach tightens a bit.

You hop into your car and turn on the radio. The same news is playing on every station: the internet is down. There's no obvious explanation why. Is it a terrorist attack? A war and invasion starting? Another, more serious HuggingFace incident? A test? A solar storm (that somehow only affected the internet and not any other critical infrastructure)? Speculation runs rampant among the talking heads.

The situation is beginning to dawn on you. You can't fill up on gas because the pumps are connected to the internet for payment. You can't get groceries because the store's database communicates with the internet and, well, payment. You can't buy anything or order from Doordash. You can't call or text or email. Your work meetings are obviously cancelled. You don't know what your girlfriend is doing. Did she sleep over at her sister's last night? All the way on the other side of town? You hope she knows the Schelling point is your apartment... wait, she's bad at navigating around town without her maps app. Can she even make her way back? Do the traffic lights even work? Didn't she say she needed to pick up her prescription today because it was running low? Shit—you were supposed to go grocery shopping today so you could stop living off of the minute-made rice and almost-week-old chicken. And now the car's kinda low on gas. Guess you gotta get back upstairs and figure this all out.

But what is there to figure out? You don't have a radio, so the only way to communicate—or at least listen—to the outside world is in your car. Cash is only so good, but the nearest grocery store is a few miles away and you have no idea how long this will last, so saving the last 50 miles of gas is probably a good idea.

Now, get out of story mode and just think about what life without the internet would be like. Spend a day noting down every time you use the internet in some form. Combine that with not knowing when the internet would come back on. So much uncertainty, so much lack of knowledge.

Precedents

Has anything like this happened in the past? What can we learn from those events? Did roaming gangs of marauders come into existence within hours of this happening, or did everyone stay calm and wait for the authorities to solve it?

I ranked them by overall harm: deaths first, then how long people lost power, phones, water, internet or payments, and how many people. Iran 2026 sits at #3 even though no deaths are attributed to the outage itself, because it's the closest match to your scenario at national scale. 1. **Hurricane Maria, Puerto Rico (2017).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_Hurricane_Maria_in_Puerto_Rico) · [Power fully restored Aug 2018](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2018/08/15/npr-nearly-year-after-maria-puerto-rico-officials-claim-power-totally-restored) - All 3.4 million residents lost power. The last customers were reconnected on August 14, 2018, nearly 11 months later. - 95% of cell networks were down at first, and 40% of the island still had no cell service a month later. - The official death toll was revised to 2,975. People were still waiting in ATM lines more than a week after the storm. - **Lesson:** a multi-week outage is a real possibility; plan for 30 days, not 3. 2. **Texas winter storm (February 2021).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Texas_power_crisis) - 4.5 million homes and businesses lost power at the peak, some for more than 3 days. - Water service was disrupted for more than 12 million people. - Official deaths were 246, and some estimates run to 700 or more. Hypothermia caused almost two-thirds; carbon monoxide caused at least 19. - **Lesson:** warmth and carbon monoxide safety kill or save more people than anything else on the list. 3. **Iran's internet blackout (January–May 2026).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Internet_blackout_in_Iran) · [Cloudflare Q1 2026 report](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q1-2026-internet-disruption-summary/) · [Partial restoration after 88 days](https://tribune.com.pk/story/2610296/greetings-after-88-days-iranians-reconnect-after-long-internet-shutdown?amp=1) - This was a government shutdown, not an accident. A near-total cut started January 8 during protests and also took down mobile and landline phones at first. Connectivity only recovered to about 50%. - A second cut on February 28, after military strikes, dropped traffic below 1% of normal for 88 days. Restoration from May 26 was only partial. - The domestic intranet kept some banking and state services running. The government put the cost at $35.7M a day. - **Lesson:** a country of about 90 million went months without the internet. "It'll be back by tomorrow" isn't guaranteed. 4. **Hurricane Helene, western North Carolina (2024).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_Hurricane_Helene_in_North_Carolina) · [FCC cell outage figures](https://www.benton.org/headlines/road-recovery-western-north-carolina) · [Asheville water back after 53 days](https://www.wunc.org/2024-11-18/asheville-taps-are-clear-to-drink-boil-water-notice-lifted) - 74% of cell sites in the disaster area were down the day after the storm, and about 9% were still out 2.5 weeks later. - Asheville had no drinkable tap water for 53 days. There were 108 deaths in North Carolina, mostly from flooding. - Starlink offered free service, but the hardware still cost about $349 and road damage blocked deliveries. Volunteer "flush brigades" carried water to people's toilets. - **Lesson:** buy Starlink and water gear before the disaster, and plan for sanitation. 5. **Northeast blackout (2003).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_blackout_of_2003) - About 55 million people in eight US states and Ontario lost power for 2 hours to 4 days, and almost 100 people died. - Cell networks overloaded while wired phones mostly worked, and 911 dropped out in 15-minute stretches. - Detroit's 4 million water customers were under a boil-water notice for several days. Many gas stations couldn't pump. - **Behavior:** people directed traffic, restaurants gave food away, and neighborhoods threw block parties. 6. **India blackouts (2012).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_India_blackouts) - 600 million or more people across 22 states lost power over two days, the largest blackout by number of people. - More than 300 trains stopped, about 200 miners were trapped underground (all rescued), and water treatment plants shut down. - **Lesson:** water pumps fail when the power does. 7. **Spain and Portugal blackout (April 2025).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Iberian_Peninsula_blackout) - Nearly the whole peninsula lost power for about 10 hours or more. Spain's internet traffic fell to 17% of normal. - Card payments stopped in Portugal and parts of Spain, all trains stopped, and about 35,000 people had to be rescued from stranded trains. - 8 people died: three from a generator run indoors, one from a candle fire, and two who relied on home oxygen or ventilator machines. - People panic-bought batteries and radios. Madrid saw both fear and festive street scenes. - **Lesson:** cash, a radio, medical-device backup power, and never running a generator indoors. 8. **Cuba grid collapse (October 2024).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024%E2%80%932026_Cuba_blackouts) - The whole national grid failed, leaving about 10 million people without power from roughly October 18 to 22. A hurricane slowed repairs. - Protests broke out in Havana, and more nationwide collapses followed in 2025–26. - **Lesson:** a fragile grid can fail again and again. 9. **New York City blackout (1977).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_blackout_of_1977) - A lightning strike cut power to most of the city for about 26 hours. - 1,616 stores were looted or damaged, there were about 3,800–4,500 arrests and 1,037 fires, and about $300M in damage. - **This is the counterexample to 2003:** the same city with the same problem behaved very differently. 10. **Tonga (2022).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Hunga_Tonga%E2%80%93Hunga_Ha%CA%BBapai_eruption_and_tsunami) - A volcanic eruption cut the country's only undersea internet cable on January 15, and the whole nation was offline for about 5 weeks. - Phone service partly returned by January 20 and a limited satellite link by January 21. The cable was repaired on February 22 after about 55 km of it was lost. - **Lesson:** this is the purest "the internet is gone for weeks" case. 11. **Iran's internet blackout (2019).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Internet_blackout_in_Iran) - A near-total nationwide shutdown lasted 8 days during protests, with connectivity down to about 5% of normal. - Domestic banking and state apps kept working. The estimated cost was $1–1.5B. 12. **Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack (2021).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Pipeline_ransomware_attack) - A cyberattack shut the main fuel pipeline to the East Coast for 6 days. - 87% of DC gas stations and 71% of Charlotte's ran dry, and panic buying made it worse. - **Lesson:** keep the gas tank above half. 13. **Change Healthcare ransomware attack (2024).** [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2024/02/23/change-healthcare-cyberattack-pharmacies) · [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/miami/news/unitedhealth-cyberattack-change-healthcare-prescription-access-still-impacted) - An attack on a pharmacy claims processor disrupted prescription processing for weeks. UnitedHealth said more than 90% of US pharmacies had to change how they processed claims. - Some patients paid cash, and military pharmacies took about 6 weeks to recover. - **Lesson:** supports the 90-day supply and "expect to pay cash" items. 14. **CrowdStrike outage (2024).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_CrowdStrike-related_IT_outages) - A faulty security update crashed 8.5 million Windows machines. Card payments failed at stores and gas stations, hospitals postponed procedures, and New Hampshire's 911 went down statewide. - Delta cancelled more than 7,000 flights over 5 days. 15. **Rogers outage, Canada (2022).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Rogers_Communications_outage) - 12 million or more customers, about 25% of Canada's internet, were out for about 15 hours. - Interac debit went down nationwide whatever your provider, mobile 911 calls failed, and one death was reported. - **Lesson:** one company's mistake can take out payments for an entire country. 16. **Optus outage, Australia (2023).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Optus_outage) - 10 million people and 400,000 businesses were cut off for about 12 hours. Some emergency (000) calls failed, card payments failed, and hundreds of trains were cancelled. 17. **Quebec geomagnetic storm (1989).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_1989_geomagnetic_storm) - A solar storm took Quebec's grid down in under 90 seconds, and about 6 million people lost power for about 9 hours. - **Lesson:** your scenario's "solar storm" isn't far-fetched. 18. **AWS outage (October 2025).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Amazon_Web_Services_outage) - A bug in one Amazon data center region took down apps worldwide for about 14 hours, including Snapchat, Reddit, Coinbase, Ring and Alexa. - **Lesson:** smart-home devices rely on the cloud and stop working without it. 19. **Ukraine power grid cyberattack (2015).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/December_2015_Ukraine_power_grid_cyberattack) - Russian hackers (Sandworm) remotely switched off 30 substations, and about 230,000 people lost power for 1–6 hours. - It was the first publicly acknowledged successful cyberattack on a power grid. The hackers also flooded the utility's call center so customers couldn't get through. 20. **Viasat satellite hack (2022).** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viasat_hack) · [DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/viasat-our-network-was-hit-by-a-multifaceted-and-deliberate-cyberattack) - On the day Russia invaded Ukraine, a cyberattack disabled tens of thousands of satellite internet modems across Europe. - About 5,800 German wind turbines lost remote control for weeks. - **Lesson:** satellite internet isn't immune either.

Practices

What to do once you think the internet is down, roughly in order.

  • First hour
    1. Confirm it's really down: restart your phone and router, then ask a neighbor or walk to a store or gas station and see whether their card readers work. A dead router or a carrier outage is far more likely than the whole internet going down, and you don't want to start rationing over a broken modem.
    2. Turn on the radio: tune to your area's main AM news station or the NOAA weather channel, and check back on the hour. Officials will use broadcast radio to say what's happening and how long it might last, and the largest AM stations are built to stay on the air in emergencies. Write your station's frequency on your paper list ahead of time.
    3. Charge everything: phones, power banks, laptops, headlamps, and the power station, while the power is still on. Whatever took out the internet may take out the grid next.
    4. Fill the bathtub and any empty containers: water systems run on electric pumps and networked controls, so pressure can drop without warning.
    5. Text, don't call: send short texts to family instead of calling, if your phone has any signal. Texts use less of the network and often get through when calls don't. If nothing goes through, fall back on your family plan: the out-of-state contact and the meeting place.
  • First day
    1. Top up what's still for sale: if a store or gas station is open and taking cash, fill the gas tank and pick up prescriptions and anything you're short on. Lines form and shelves empty within hours, so go early, and buy what you need rather than a cart of everything.
    2. Check on neighbors: especially older or disabled ones, and anyone who lives alone. They may not have a radio, and you'll want each other's help if this lasts.
    3. Save your phone battery: turn on low power mode, lower the brightness, and switch to airplane mode when you don't need it. A phone searching for a signal drains its battery fast, so turn it on at set times, like on the hour, to check for service.
    4. Eat from the fridge first: if the power is out too, eat what's in the fridge, then the freezer, then the pantry, and keep the doors shut. Food in a closed fridge stays safe for about 4 hours; a full freezer, about 48 (USDA).
    5. Unplug electronics if the power goes out: everything but one lamp, which tells you when it's back. A surge when the power returns can damage whatever's plugged in.
  • Until it's back
    1. Assume it'll last: plan for at least two weeks until the radio says otherwise. Use water and fuel as if you won't get more, but keep eating normally; you need the calories, and the food is there for this.
    2. Drive only when you have to: treat dead traffic lights as four-way stops, which is the law in most states. Gas is hard to replace, and intersections without lights are where outages turn into crashes.
    3. Keep washing your hands: before eating and after using the toilet bucket. Diarrheal illness is the main health risk once water and sanitation break down.
    4. Keep a paper log: write down radio updates, which stores and stations are open, and when. Without a phone, nothing else will remember it for you.

Tier 1

Estimated cost for your household: $310, plus the cash itself.

  1. Cash.
    • Amount: $500-2,000 per household, or a few weeks of normal spending.
    • Small bills: mostly 1s, 5s, 10s, and 20s. Stores may not be able to make change, and nobody will break a $100.
    • Why it's first: card readers, ATMs, and payment apps all need a network. Cash is the only way to pay that works offline.
  2. Water, 2 weeks.
    • Stored water 14 gal per person: 1 gallon per person per day for up to two weeks, in 7-gallon jugs like the Reliance Aqua-Tainer or in cases of bottled water. About half is for drinking; the rest is for cooking and washing.
    • Filter 1 per household: a squeeze filter like the Sawyer Squeeze (~$40) or a gravity filter like the Platypus GravityWorks. Once stored water runs out, it turns tub, creek, or rain water into drinking water. Filters remove bacteria and parasites, not chemicals.
    • Unscented bleach 1 bottle per household: regular household bleach (5-9% sodium hypochlorite, not scented or "splash-less") as a backup for treating water: 8 drops per gallon of clear water, 16 if it's cloudy, then wait 30 minutes (CDC). Bleach loses strength over time, so replace it yearly. The same bottle doubles as disinfectant.
    • Bathtub: fill it the moment you hear about an outage, since water pressure can fail if the utility's pumps or controls go down. Use it for flushing and washing; for drinking, filter it or use a tub liner like the WaterBOB (~100 gallons).
    • Why not store 30 days: a full 30 days is ~250 lb of water per person, so past two weeks the filter is the better plan.
    • Rotation: refill containers you filled from the tap every 6 months, and replace store-bought bottles by their date (CDC). Keep both out of sunlight.
  3. Food, 2 weeks of things you already eat. The amounts are a rough guide at 2,000 calories per person per day; swap in what your household actually eats.
    • Canned goods 14 cans per person: soup, chili, beans, tuna, chicken, vegetables, and fruit. They're already cooked, so you can eat them cold if you can't cook.
    • Peanut butter 1 jar per person: about 2,700 calories per 16 oz jar, with protein and fat, no cooking, and months of shelf life.
    • Oats 1 canister per person: rolled or quick oats. Quick oats soften in cold water or milk if you can't cook.
    • Pasta 3 lb per person: cheap calories, but it takes a lot of water and fuel to cook.
    • Rice 3 lb per person: white rice keeps for years; brown rice goes rancid in months.
    • Shelf-stable milk 4 quarts per person: boxed UHT milk (e.g. Horizon or Parmalat) or powdered milk, which keeps for months unopened without a fridge.
    • Ready-to-eat snacks 3 boxes per person: granola or protein bars, crackers, trail mix, and jerky. No prep, and good for morale.
    • Coffee or tea, if you drink it: instant coffee works without a coffee maker, and caffeine withdrawal headaches are miserable.
    • Manual can opener 1 per household: a hand-crank one like the OXO Good Grips (~$15). Electric openers won't work, and not every can has a pull tab.
    • Skip 25-year freeze-dried buckets: they're expensive, and you'll never rotate them.
  4. Medicine. Full list below.
    • Prescriptions: a 90-day supply of anything you take daily (ask your doctor; many insurers allow it). Pharmacies may not be able to process refills during an outage.
    • Basic OTC kit: pain reliever, allergy pills, diarrhea medicine, and bandages to start.
    • Spare glasses or contacts: an old pair of glasses or an extra box of contacts. You can't order replacements, and losing your only pair can mean you can't drive.
    • Expiration dates: expired OTC pills mostly keep their potency, so don't stress about dates.
  5. Light, power, and alerts.
    • Headlamps 1 per person: e.g. a Petzl Tikka or Black Diamond Spot (~$30-50). Hands-free light for cooking, first aid, and stairwells; a building's emergency lights are only required to last about 90 minutes.
    • AA/AAA batteries 1 pack of 24 per person: enough to replace everything twice. Lithium ones like Energizer Ultimate Lithium last up to 20 years in storage and work better in the cold.
    • Power banks 1 per person (at least 2): 10,000-20,000 mAh each (e.g. Anker), kept charged. A 10,000 mAh bank charges a typical phone about twice.
    • Radio 1 per household: a hand-crank or battery AM/FM/NOAA weather radio, such as the Midland ER310 or Eton FRX3 (~$50-70). When the internet is down, broadcast radio is how officials will reach you.
    • Emergency alerts: make sure emergency alerts are turned on in your phone's settings, and sign up for your county's text alerts (Ready.gov explains how). They're how officials reach you while phones still work.
  6. Paper. Keep these in a waterproof folder.
    • Phone numbers: family, friends, doctors, your landlord, your employer, and an out-of-state contact. Most people know two or three numbers by heart.
    • Radio frequencies: your area's main AM news station and an FM backup (look up your ZIP code on Radio-Locator), and which of the seven NOAA weather channels covers you (find your county on the National Weather Service's list). You can't look them up once the internet is down.
    • Printed local map: a road atlas or printouts, with the places to know marked.
    • Copies of IDs: driver's license, passport, and birth certificate. They prove who you are to banks, pharmacies, and shelters when their systems can't look you up.
    • Insurance info: health insurance cards (front and back), plus your renter's or home and car policy numbers and claims phone numbers.
    • Account numbers: bank, credit cards, and utilities, with their phone numbers, so you can sort things out when systems come back.
    • Medication list: your medications, doses, allergies, and doctors' numbers. Keep one copy in the med kit and one in your wallet.
    • Why paper: all of this lives on your phone, which is a single point of failure that'll be dead or useless within days.

Tier 2

Estimated cost for your household: $1,920. Starlink and the minimal power setup are about $1,450 of that.

  1. Extend food past two weeks. Staples for the rest of your plan after Tier 1's two weeks, at 2,000 calories per person per day:
    • White rice 8 lb per person: about 1,650 calories per pound, the cheapest calories that store for years.
    • Dried beans or lentils 5 lb per person: about 1,550 calories per pound, plus the protein and fiber rice lacks. Lentils cook in 20-30 minutes without soaking, which saves fuel.
    • Oats 3 lb per person: about 1,700 calories per pound of breakfast.
    • Peanut butter 2 jars per person: 16 oz jars, about 2,700 calories each.
    • Cooking oil 1 quart per person: about 7,700 calories per quart. Rice and beans alone are very lean, and oil is the densest way to add calories.
    • Salt 1 canister per household: a 26 oz canister makes rice and beans edible and replaces what you sweat out.
    • Sugar 1 bag (4 lb) per household: quick calories that keep forever.
    • Canned vegetables 6 cans per person: for vitamins and variety.
    • Canned meat or fish 6 cans per person: tuna, chicken, salmon, or Spam. Protein that's already cooked.
    • Multivitamin 16 tablets per person: one a day covers what a rice-and-beans diet lacks, such as vitamin C.
    • Rotation: buy what you'll actually eat in normal cooking, and use the oldest first so nothing expires on the shelf.
  2. Internet: Starlink Mini.
    • What it does: gives you internet from satellites when local fiber and cell towers are both down.
    • Hardware 1 per household: ~$199, varies by promotion.
    • Standby mode: ~$5-10/month depending on the source, at roughly 0.5 Mbps. Enough for messaging, maps, and reactivating full service, and it means you aren't trying to buy a plan in the middle of an outage.
    • Roam plan: full speed only when you need it, roughly $50+/month.
    • Sky view: it needs a clear view of the sky from a balcony or window. The Starlink app checks for obstructions; test it now, not during an outage.
    • Lease: check your lease before mounting anything outside.
    • Power: it draws about 25-40 W. It reportedly runs from a 100 W USB-C PD power bank, though I haven't tested this.
    • Pricing: it changes often. Check starlink.com; sources: Notebookcheck, Basenor.
  3. Power, minimal (~$1,250). Runs Starlink Mini and phones.
    • Daily need: ~1 kWh: Starlink Mini 0.7 (about 30 W all day), phones and laptops 0.2. Running Starlink only a few hours a day cuts it a lot.
    • Battery 1 per household: a 1 kWh LiFePO4 power station, e.g. the EcoFlow DELTA 3 Plus. LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate) lasts thousands of charge cycles and is less fire-prone than other lithium batteries, which matters indoors.
    • Solar panel 1 per household: one 400 W folding panel, which refills ~1 kWh on a sunny day.
    • Apartment catch: the panel needs direct sun on a balcony; light through window glass gives much less.
  4. Cooking without power.
    • Stove 1 per household: a single-burner butane stove (e.g. Gas One or Iwatani, ~$25-90) or a camping canister stove.
    • Fuel 22 canisters per household: 8 oz butane canisters. Each lasts about 1.5-2 hours on high.
    • Ventilation: crack a window. Any flame indoors makes carbon monoxide.
    • Never indoors: charcoal grills and generators, including on an enclosed balcony. Carbon monoxide poisoning killed people during the Texas freeze and kills people in nearly every major outage.
    • Lease: check it. Many ban open flames or fuel storage.
  5. Sanitation. People forget this one. If the water stops, you can still flush by pouring a gallon or two (tub water works) straight into the bowl. If it stops for long, you need a backup toilet, and waste becomes a health problem within days.
    • Heavy trash bags 1 box of 20 per person: contractor bags, 3 mil or thicker, in boxes of 20. They line the toilet bucket and hold trash if pickup stops.
    • 5-gallon bucket 1 per household: with a snap-on toilet seat lid, like the Reliance Luggable Loo (~$20-30). The emergency toilet.
    • Kitty litter or sawdust 1 bag per person: 20-40 lb bags. A scoop after each use controls smell and moisture.
    • Toilet paper 9 rolls per person: more than you think.
    • Soap 1 bar per person: bar or liquid. Handwashing is the main defense against the diarrheal illnesses that spread when sanitation breaks down.
    • Wipes 3 packs of 80 per person: unscented baby wipes, for washing yourself when you can't shower. Don't flush them.
    • Hand sanitizer 1 bottle per person: at least 60% alcohol, for when there's no water to wash with.
  6. Warmth and safety.
    • Sleeping bag or heavy blankets 1 per person: a sleeping bag rated to 20-30°F for each person, or wool blankets. Without heat, a home can get dangerously cold in a day or two; hypothermia was the leading cause of death in the 2021 Texas freeze, and ice storms kill more people than collapse scenarios.
    • Fire extinguisher 1 per household: an ABC-rated one, 5 lb or larger (e.g. First Alert or Kidde). Candles, stoves, and space heaters start fires, and the fire department may be slow to arrive.
    • CO detector 1 per household: battery-powered, or plug-in with a battery backup, since a plug-in alone dies with the power. Stoves, generators, and cars running near the building all make carbon monoxide, which you can't smell.
    • Gas tank above half: keep it there from now on. Pumps need power and a network, and your car is also a radio, a charger, and a heater.
  7. Pets and babies, if applicable.
    • Pet food 5 weeks' supply per pet: what they already eat, since a sudden switch upsets their stomachs. Plus water: a dog needs about an ounce per pound per day.
    • Litter 3 bags per cat: the kind your cat already uses.
    • Pet medications, vaccination records, and a carrier or leash 1 per pet: shelters that take pets often require records and crates.
    • Formula 5 weeks' supply per baby: your usual brand. Ready-to-feed formula needs no clean water, which matters if the taps stop.
    • Diapers and wipes 240 diapers per baby: in the current size and the next size up, since babies grow.
  8. Morale. Long outages are boring and stressful, especially for kids.
    • Paper books 2 books per person: a few you've been meaning to read. E-readers and tablets die with the power.
    • Cards and board games 1 per household: a deck of cards, Uno, and a couple of board games. Something to do together after dark.
    • For kids 1 per kid: coloring books, crayons, puzzles, and a favorite small toy. Something familiar to do helps them cope.
    • Treats 2 bags per person: chocolate, candy, or whatever your household's comfort food is. Small comforts go a long way when everything else is hard.
    • Notebooks and pens 1 per household: for the paper log, lists, and notes to neighbors.

Tier 3

Estimated cost for your household: $5,400. The bigger power setup is about $5,000 of that.

  1. Offline library.
    • Wikipedia isn't a manual: it's great for browsing but poor for emergencies, so don't make it your main resource.
    • Kiwix: a free app for reading downloaded websites offline. Get English Wikipedia without images (~50 GB, check current size), plus the medical subset (WikiMed).
    • Offline maps: Organic Maps or OsmAnd, with your state or region downloaded ahead of time. Both navigate by GPS, which works without internet or cell service.
    • Printed pages: the 20 that matter: first aid, water purification (the EPA and CDC both have one-page guides), how to shut off your home's water and gas, and a few staple recipes. Better yet, a paper copy of Where There Is No Doctor, which Hesperian also offers as a free PDF.
    • Local LLM (optional): a model run through Ollama on a laptop; an 8B-parameter model runs on most laptops with 16 GB of RAM. Decent offline Q&A, but it drains the battery fast, and verify anything medical.
  2. Tools.
    • Multitool 1 per household: e.g. a Leatherman or Victorinox. Pliers, knife, screwdrivers, and a can opener in one.
    • Duct tape 1 roll per household: patches torn tarps, leaky hoses, and broken windows.
    • Work gloves 1 pair per adult: leather, for debris and broken glass.
    • Zip ties 1 pack per household: assorted sizes, for quick fixes.
    • Flashlight 1 per household: a bright handheld one for searching and signaling. Buy one that takes the same batteries as your headlamps, so one stash powers everything.
    • Small toolkit 1 per household: screwdrivers, an adjustable wrench, a hammer, and pliers. If you have gas, keep a 12-inch wrench by the shutoff valve; only turn it off if you smell gas, since only the utility should turn it back on.
    • Small solar panel 1 per household: a 50-100 W folding USB panel keeps phones and power banks charged without the big power station.
  3. Manual hand pump or siphon.
    • Jiggle siphon 1 per household (~$10-15): moves water from the tub into jugs, or fuel between cans, without sucking on a hose. Most newer cars have anti-siphon screens, so don't count on pulling gas out of one.
  4. Power, better (~$5,000). Adds a fridge to the minimal setup.
    • Daily need: ~2.5 kWh: fridge 1.5, Starlink Mini 0.7, phones and laptops 0.2. Without power, food in a closed fridge is only safe for about 4 hours, and the fridge is also where insulin lives.
    • Battery 1 per household: a 4 kWh LiFePO4 power station, e.g. the EcoFlow DELTA Pro 3.
    • Solar 3 panels per household: 400 W folding panels, which together refill ~3 kWh/day in sun.
    • Generator 1 per household: a ~2,500 W dual-fuel inverter generator, which refills the battery in a few hours even in bad weather. "Inverter" means clean power that's safe for electronics; "dual-fuel" means it runs on gasoline or propane.
    • Propane 3 tanks per household: 20 lb tanks. Unlike gasoline, which goes stale in a few months, propane keeps for years.
    • Apartment catch: many leases and fire codes ban generators and propane storage, and you can't run a generator indoors or on a closed balcony. This tier fits a house better than an apartment.
  5. Ammunition. Only if you already own a firearm and are trained with it.
    • Ammunition: a few boxes for each firearm you own, stored cool and dry.
    • Secure storage: a lockbox or gun safe, so no one else can get to it.

Full Medicine List

Details for the Medicine item in Tier 1. This isn't medical advice: follow label doses, check interactions with your own prescriptions, and tell your doctor or pharmacist what you're stocking.

Estimated cost for your household: $380, not counting prescriptions.

  • Prescriptions
    • 90-day supply of every daily medication: ask your doctor to write 90-day prescriptions; many insurers cover them, especially by mail order.
    • Backup inhaler, if you use one: keep one at home and one in your bag. Smoke, cold air, and stress all trigger attacks.
    • Epinephrine auto-injectors, if prescribed: EpiPen or a generic. Keep two, since a reaction can come back after the first dose wears off.
    • Insulin and glucose strips, if you use them: plus lancets, pen needles, and glucose tablets for lows.
    • Note: insulin and some other drugs need cold storage, so plan for a power outage. Many insulins keep up to 28 days at room temperature (59-86°F) once out of the fridge (FDA; check your label), and an evaporative cooling wallet like a Frio keeps them cool without power. Ask about early refills now.
  • Pain and fever
    • Acetaminophen 1 bottle per household (Tylenol): fever and pain. Easier on the stomach than ibuprofen, but hard on the liver in high doses.
    • Ibuprofen or naproxen 1 bottle per household (Advil, Aleve): pain, fever, and swelling. Naproxen lasts 8-12 hours per dose.
    • Low-dose aspirin 1 bottle per household (81 mg): mostly for a suspected heart attack, when chewing four tablets (325 mg) on the way to an ER can help, unless you're allergic or your doctor has told you not to.
    • Note: don't stack acetaminophen products; many cold medicines and some Midol already contain it. Avoid NSAIDs with kidney disease, ulcers, or blood thinners. No aspirin for children or teens.
  • Allergy
    • Diphenhydramine 1 box per household (Benadryl): fast relief for hives and allergic reactions. It also makes you drowsy, so it doubles as a sleep aid.
    • Cetirizine or loratadine 1 box per household (Zyrtec, Claritin): non-drowsy, once a day, for ongoing allergies.
    • Hydrocortisone cream 1 tube per household (1%): rashes, bug bites, poison ivy, and itching.
    • Note: diphenhydramine is the fast one for reactions. Anaphylaxis needs epinephrine and 911 if phones work.
  • Stomach and gut
    • Loperamide 1 box per household (Imodium): slows diarrhea so you lose less fluid.
    • Oral rehydration salts 4 packets per person (e.g. DripDrop or WHO-formula ORS packets): replace the salt and water that diarrhea drains, and the most important item in this group. In a pinch, mix 6 level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt into a liter of clean water.
    • Electrolyte packets 7 packets per person (e.g. Liquid I.V. or LMNT): for heat, sweat, and hard work. ORS is better for illness.
    • Bismuth subsalicylate 1 bottle per household (Pepto-Bismol): upset stomach, nausea, and mild diarrhea. It can turn your tongue and stool black, which is harmless.
    • Calcium carbonate antacid 1 bottle per household (Tums): quick heartburn relief.
    • Famotidine 1 box per household (Pepcid): longer-lasting heartburn relief, up to 12 hours.
    • Polyethylene glycol 1 bottle per household (MiraLAX): constipation, which is common when your diet switches to rice and canned food.
    • Meclizine 1 box per household (Bonine, Dramamine Less Drowsy): nausea from motion sickness or vertigo.
    • Note: dehydration from diarrhea is the main danger in an outage. ORS matters more than loperamide. Skip loperamide if there's blood in the stool or a fever. Bismuth subsalicylate is a salicylate, so the aspirin cautions apply: not for children or teens, or anyone on blood thinners or allergic to aspirin.
  • Cold and respiratory
    • Saline nasal spray 1 bottle per household (e.g. Simply Saline): congestion, and the dry air that comes with running heaters.
    • Guaifenesin 1 box per household (Mucinex): loosens chest mucus so coughs clear it.
    • Pseudoephedrine 1 box per household (Sudafed, the kind behind the pharmacy counter; bring ID): the decongestant that works. The phenylephrine version on open shelves doesn't work as a decongestant, according to the FDA, which proposed removing it in 2024.
    • Cough drops 1 bag per household (e.g. Halls or Ricola): sore throat and cough.
    • Note: pseudoephedrine raises blood pressure; skip it if you have hypertension.
  • Wounds and skin
    • Antibiotic ointment 1 tube per household (Polysporin or Neosporin): keeps minor wounds moist and clean. Polysporin skips neomycin, which some people are allergic to.
    • Sterile gauze 1 box per household: 4x4 pads and a roll or two, for covering wounds and pressing on bleeding.
    • Adhesive bandages 1 box per household: assorted sizes. Fabric ones stay on better.
    • Medical tape 1 roll per household: paper or cloth, to hold gauze in place.
    • Elastic wrap 1 per household (ACE bandage): sprains, and holding dressings on.
    • Saline or clean water 1 per household, with an irrigation syringe or squeeze bottle: enough pressure to flush dirt out of a wound.
    • Povidone-iodine 1 bottle per household (Betadine): kills germs on the skin around a wound.
    • Burn gel 1 per household (e.g. Water-Jel): for minor burns, after 20 minutes under cool running water. Get care for burns bigger than your palm, or on the face, hands, or groin.
    • Tweezers 1 per household: fine-point, for splinters, glass, and ticks.
    • Trauma shears 1 per household: cut clothing away from an injury without cutting the person.
    • Tourniquet 1 per household: a CAT or SOF-T, bought from a reputable seller since counterfeits are common. It stops life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg.
    • Note: irrigating a wound well does more than any ointment. A tourniquet only helps if you know how to use it; a Stop the Bleed course teaches it in an hour or two, often for free.
  • Eyes and mouth
    • Lubricating eye drops 1 bottle per household (e.g. Systane or Refresh): dry eyes from heat, smoke, or wearing contacts too long.
    • Eyewash 1 bottle per household: sterile saline, for flushing out dust or chemicals.
    • Contact solution: a full bottle and a spare case. Never rinse contacts in tap or filtered water; it can cause a serious eye infection.
    • Temporary dental filling kit 1 per household (e.g. Dentemp) and clove oil: the kit covers a lost filling or cap, and clove oil numbs tooth pain.
    • Note: dental pain with swelling or fever needs real care; the kit only buys time.
  • Monitoring
    • Digital thermometer 1 per household: tells a mild illness from a fever that needs care.
    • Pulse oximeter 1 per household (fingertip, ~$20-30): measures blood oxygen. Readings that stay below about 92% are a reason to get to a doctor. They read less accurately on darker skin.
    • Blood pressure cuff 1 per household: an automatic upper-arm cuff (e.g. Omron); wrist cuffs are less accurate. Most useful if you take blood pressure medication and run short.
    • Glucose meter, if diabetic: plus spare strips and lancets.
    • Note: batteries for each. Write down your normal readings so you can tell when something's off.
  • Hygiene and protection
    • Nitrile gloves 1 box of 100 per household: they protect you and the wound during first aid, and your hands when emptying the toilet bucket. Nitrile rather than latex avoids latex allergies.
    • N95 masks 5 masks per person: NIOSH-approved, for smoke, dust, mold cleanup, and illness.
    • Insect repellent 1 bottle per household: 20-30% DEET or picaridin, for mosquitoes and ticks.
    • Sunscreen 1 bottle per household: SPF 30 or higher. Without a car or transit, you'll spend more time walking outside.
    • Menstrual products: tampons, pads, or a menstrual cup, plus Midol or ibuprofen for cramps. A cup is reusable, so it never runs out. Midol versions contain acetaminophen, ibuprofen, or naproxen, so count them toward those doses.

Antibiotics: don't self-prescribe. If you want a backup course for a long outage, ask your doctor directly. Some will write one, many won't.

Shelf life: most solid tablets keep most of their potency well past the printed date if stored cool, dry, and dark, but ask your pharmacist about yours. Replace on schedule: epinephrine auto-injectors, insulin, liquid medicines, nitroglycerin, and anything refrigerated.

Places to Know Nearby

Find these now, while you still have a maps app, and mark them on your printed map. Note their hours and a walking route to each: you may be short on gas, and you won't be able to look anything up. The "find nearest" links search Google Maps near you, and the PDF checklist has space to write each one down.

  • Hospital emergency room (find nearest): if phones are down, you can't call 911, so the ER is where you go instead. Hospitals have backup generators and their own radio links to ambulances, so they're among the last places to stop working, though an outage will slow them down (hospitals hit by ransomware have had to go back to paper). Know the nearest one, a second in case the first is overwhelmed or turning ambulances away, and which is a trauma center.
  • Fire and police stations (find nearest fire or police): they're staffed around the clock and talk over their own radio systems, which don't depend on the internet or cell towers. If you can't call 911, you can walk in to report a fire or medical emergency and have help radioed out. Fire crews may be out on calls, so a police station is the better bet if the firehouse is empty. Both are likely places to find official news.
  • Pharmacy (find nearest): prescriptions are the one thing you can't improvise. Pharmacies check insurance online, so expect to pay cash and bring your printed medication list. Many states let pharmacists give a short emergency supply without reaching your doctor. Know your usual pharmacy and an independent one, which may be more willing to work on paper.
  • Grocery stores (find nearest): know two or three, including one within walking distance. Big chains run on networked registers and card payments, while a small independent or ethnic market may be more willing to take cash and keep a paper tab. Shelves empty fast, which is why your own food comes first.
  • Gas stations (find nearest): pumps need power and card readers need a network. Know which nearby stations take cash at the counter and which have backup generators; after hurricanes, those are the ones that stay open. Gas keeps your car working as a radio, a phone charger, a heater, and a way out.
  • Bank or credit union branch (find nearest): ATMs and cards will likely be down, and a branch might still hand out cash against your ID if it opens at all. Know where yours is, but don't count on it: that's why cash is item 1.
  • Shelters, warming centers, and cooling centers: usually schools, libraries, community centers, and churches your county designates ahead of time. They offer heat or air conditioning, water, charging, and news. In large outages, volunteer ham radio operators often set up at shelters and hospitals to pass messages, which may be your only way to reach family out of town. Your county's emergency management page lists the usual sites; write them down now.
  • Hardware store (find nearest): batteries, propane, tarps, water containers, and generators sell out first. Know where the nearest one is for whatever you forgot, but buy ahead rather than counting on it.
  • Nearest fresh water (find nearest): a creek, lake, or pond is what your filter is for if the taps stop. Filters handle bacteria and parasites but not chemicals, so pick a source away from roads, farms, and industry, and boil or bleach the water too if you can.
  • Your family's meeting place: see For Your Family. Mark a walking route to it, since not everyone can find their way without a maps app.

Upkeep

  • Food: eat and replace anything near its date, oldest first.

For Your Family

The same list works for family. Add two things that cost nothing:

  • A shared plan: one out-of-state contact everyone calls or texts, and a physical meeting place if phones are down. When local lines jam, a long-distance call often gets through when a call across town won't.
  • A shared checklist: a spreadsheet or printed page showing who has what, so you're not all buying the same things.

A Boilerplate Message You Can Send to Family and Friends

Here's a simple message that you can send to non-technical, unaware family and friends to encourage them to prepare themselves:

Hey [person/man/dude/bro/brah/bruh/girl/girlie],

I know this may sound a bit weird and sci-fi, but I think you should consider preparing for the internet going down for an extended period of time in the near future. Open-source AI programs are getting much better, making it easier for bad guys to hack critical infrastructure like the internet, but also water systems and the electrical grid.

I recommend either visiting this page ([FINISH]) for suggestions or asking your own Claude/ChatGPT/Gemini for recommendations about specifics your situation. Or both! It's pretty low-cost to do all of that and you can structure things so you'll use them at some point.
