You Are Not Prepared For the Internet Going Down

Published

Preparation isn't nearly as costly as the alternative. Written in conjunction with Claude Opus 5.5

Contents

A Scenario

You wake up and roll over for your phone to check last night's situational happenings on the Twitter timeline. It isn't loading. You check another app and that isn't loading either. It's then that you notice the "SOS" on the top right. No three Wi-Fi bars, no LTE, no 5G. Just a simple "SOS". Hmm, that's weird. You restart your phone, but no dice—those three pesky letters are taunting you, standing in the way of you and your informative morning routine.

The same is true for the laptop and the television. You knock on your neighbor's door and ask if you can borrow their phone to check on your girlfriend, but theirs is also out. The knot in your stomach tightens a bit.

You hop into your car and turn on the radio. The same news is playing on every station: the internet is down. There's no obvious explanation why. Is it a terrorist attack? Another, more serious HuggingFace incident? A test? A solar storm (that somehow only affected the internet and not any other critical infrastructure)? Speculation runs rampant.

The situation is beginning to dawn on you. You can't fill up on gas. You can't get groceries. You can't buy anything or order in. You can't call or text or email. Your work meetings are cancelled. You don't know what your girlfriend is doing. Did she sleep over at her sister's last night? All the way on that side of town? You hope she knows the Schelling point is your apartment... wait, she's bad at navigating from there. Can she even make her way back? Do the traffic lights even work? Didn't she say she needed to pick up her prescription today because it was running dangerously low? Shit—you were supposed to go grocery shopping today so you could stop living off of the minute-made rice and sketchy chicken. And now the car's kinda low on gas. Guess I gotta get back upstairs and figure this all out.

But what is there to figure out? You don't have a radio, so the only way to communicate—or at least listen—to the outside world is in your car. Cash is only so good, but the nearest grocery store is a few miles away and you have no idea how long this will last, so saving the last 90 miles of gas is probably a good idea.

Now, get out of story mode and just think about what life without the internet would be like. Combine that with not knowing when the internet would come back on. So much uncertainty, so much lack of knowledge.

Precedents
