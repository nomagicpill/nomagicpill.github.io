First Things First: Do My Titles Suck?

My guess is yes70%. My naming them was never optimized for maximum engagement—I just wanted something simple. I was also incentivized to keep it short so my subtitle/description could be longer, as I have a rule that posts could only take up a single line on my previous index page.

So to test my theory, I made a CSV of all of my titles and subtitles (or data-description attributes in each a href tag in the index file) at the time of writing: titles.csv. I fed them into some LLMs and the minds of a few friends to get their thoughts with the simple prompt of:

Column A is a title and column B is the a subtitle. Which column is better?

Robotic Thoughts

(I paraphrase for length purposes.)

Claude Opus 4.8 is straightforward, likely due to my custom instructions that include my abiding by Crocker's Rules:

Subtitles.

Your titles are mostly doing the job of a filing label, and a large fraction of them are the weakest title form there is: a single abstract noun. Agency. Distractions. Priorities. Regret. Empty. Ruined. Boredom. Competition. Eulogies. These have no friction — they name a topic without making a claim or creating an image, so they could sit on top of anyone's essay. The subtitle is what rescues them. "Regret" tells me nothing; "My ongoing relationship with regret and how to reduce its future impact" tells me there's a person, a stance, and a payoff.

Travel posts. The title is a proper noun and literally cannot carry information beyond geography. Iceland. Taiwan. Montreal. Uganda. All the work — voice, hook, the whole reason to click — is in the subtitle: "Green and black sprinkled with trust and horses," "Shortlisted for Earth's top utopian society," "Nature is both peaceful and metal, just not at the same time." Here the subtitle isn't better, it's the entire post-title. Riding with Death at 50 mph | His whispers sound like gale force winds is the one travel entry where the title competes, and notice why: the title finally makes a move instead of naming a place.

Essays with a thesis-as-title. This is your strongest title work and the only category where titles consistently win: Modern Life is War. Nobody Cares. Stop Rewarding Beginner Success. Peters Hide Amongst Us and Must Be Exposed. My American Dream Is Choosing Where to Live. These have a verb or a collision or a stated claim. They don't need the subtitle to function.

So the real finding: your subtitles have a consistent voice and your titles don't. If I stripped one column and read only the survivor, the subtitle column reads like you — funny, specific, opinionated ("Fur babies must be stopped for the future of our regular babies," "Wielding inconvenience as a weapon against fat," "It's not that they got there, it's that they stay there"). The title column reads like a table of contents someone else generated from your posts.

Human Thoughts

What Makes a Good Title Anyway?

It's catchy. Engaging. Makes some passerby stop their scrolling or thumb from going over the X button. Elicits some strong reaction in either good or bad ways. Not clickbait. Funny (at times). Informative and descriptive.

I suppose it also depends on the piece itself. Is it meant to be raw info in a repository-like format? Boring is fine and likely good. Is it a strong claim that readers should respond to? Put that assertion in the title. Is it a reflection? Be vulnerable and open about it from the get-go.
