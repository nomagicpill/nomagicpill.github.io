Your Subtitle Should Be Your Main Title and You Should Throw Your Main Title in the Trash

After a conversation with a mayor at LessOnline.

First Things First: Do My Titles Suck?

My guess is yes70%. My naming them was never optimized for maximum engagement—I just wanted something simple. I was also incentivized to keep it short so my subtitle/description could be longer, as I only allowed posts to take up a single line on my previous index page.

Here's a CSV of all of my titles and subtitles (or data-description attributes in each a href tag in the index file) at the time of writing: titles.csv. I fed them into both some LLMs and the minds of a few friends to get their thoughts with the simple prompt of:

Column A is a title and column B is the a subtitle. Which column is better?

Robotic Thoughts

Claude Opus 4.8 is straightforward, likely due to my custom instructions that include my abiding by Crocker's Rules:

Subtitles. It's not close, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of a few good ones.

Your titles are mostly doing the job of a filing label, and a large fraction of them are the weakest title form there is: a single abstract noun. Agency. Distractions. Priorities. Regret. Empty. Ruined. Boredom. Competition. Eulogies. These have no friction — they name a topic without making a claim or creating an image, so they could sit on top of anyone's essay. The subtitle is what rescues them. "Regret" tells me nothing; "My ongoing relationship with regret and how to reduce its future impact" tells me there's a person, a stance, and a payoff.

The split is sharpest in three places, and noticing the pattern tells you more than a verdict does:

Travel posts. The title is a proper noun and literally cannot carry information beyond geography. Iceland. Taiwan. Montreal. Uganda. All the work — voice, hook, the whole reason to click — is in the subtitle: "Green and black sprinkled with trust and horses," "Shortlisted for Earth's top utopian society," "Nature is both peaceful and metal, just not at the same time." Here the subtitle isn't better, it's the entire post-title. Riding with Death at 50 mph | His whispers sound like gale force winds is the one travel entry where the title competes, and notice why: the title finally makes a move instead of naming a place.

List/reference posts. Here it inverts. Quotes, Links, Notes I, Puzzles — the title carries because the category is the content, and the subtitle goes slack ("vibes," "Random thoughts"). When the post is an index, a plain label is correct and the subtitle is dead weight.

Essays with a thesis-as-title. This is your strongest title work and the only category where titles consistently win: Modern Life is War. Nobody Cares. Stop Rewarding Beginner Success. Peters Hide Amongst Us and Must Be Exposed. My American Dream Is Choosing Where to Live. These have a verb or a collision or a stated claim. They don't need the subtitle to function.

So the real finding: your subtitles have a consistent voice and your titles don't. If I stripped one column and read only the survivor, the subtitle column reads like you — funny, specific, opinionated ("Fur babies must be stopped for the future of our regular babies," "Wielding inconvenience as a weapon against fat," "It's not that they got there, it's that they stay there"). The title column reads like a table of contents someone else generated from your posts.

The actionable version, if you want it: your titles are good exactly when they steal what the subtitles do — make a claim, use a verb, force a concrete image. The abstract-noun titles are the ones to attack. You already proved you can do it; "Modern Life is War" and "Nobody Cares" are in the same list as "Empty" and "Boredom."

One hunch, unverified: I'd bet the subtitles came easier to you and the titles felt like a chore, because the subtitles sound improvised and alive while a chunk of the titles sound dutiful. If that's true, the fix is to let a punchy subtitle get promoted to title whenever the current title is just a topic word.

Gemini 3.5 Flash is a bit more cautious in insulting my title-naming abilities:

To determine whether Column A (Title) or Column B (Subtitle) is "better," we have to look at what they are trying to accomplish. After analyzing the contents of the dataset, here is a breakdown of how both columns perform:

Column A functions as a structured anchor.

  • The Style: It uses clean, high-level conceptual framing, often relying on multi-part series naming conventions (e.g., Identity I, Identity II, Pacing I, Discipline I)[cite: 1].
  • The Strength: Excellent for organization, categorization, and creating a cohesive theme or brand across a large volume of essays or chapters.
  • The Weakness: On its own, it can feel a bit clinical or abstract (e.g., "Discipline IV: Digging Graves" doesn't tell you exactly what you're getting into)[cite: 1, 2].

Column B acts as the hook and the context.

  • The Style: Poetic, punchy, and grounded in real-world application (e.g., "Unproductive time removed ≠ productive time gained" or "Strangers to friends in an instant")[cite: 1, 2].
  • The Strength: This column does the heavy lifting for reader engagement. It injects philosophy, tone, and immediate clarity into what the piece is actually about.
  • The Weakness: Without Column A, these lines would feel scattered, disconnected, and a bit like a collection of disparate thoughts rather than a unified body of work.

If you are judging purely on literary intrigue and engagement, Column B is better. It contains the actual "meat" of the ideas, the voice of the writer, and the emotional hook that makes a reader want to keep reading.

However, they are ultimately designed to be a team: Column A builds the house, and Column B furnishes it.
