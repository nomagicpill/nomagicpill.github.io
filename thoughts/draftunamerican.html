Buying American to Buy American is Un-American

Competition should be in, nepotism should be out. (This goes for buying anything.)

Contents

Consumer Nullification

Capitalism is built upon competition that ultimately drives improvements in all categories, from price reductions to quality to safety. Slowly but surely everything gets better as consumers jump ship from product A to product B after a change, then back to product A after it's improved to compete with product B.

This race to being the best is effectively nullified when consumers choose to buy based on something other than the overall attractiveness of the product, creating the capitalistic version of jury nullification. They don't care about the product being good, they just care about the cause of #BuyAmerican or #SupportAVeteran or #WomanOwned or whatever else is distracting people from the fact that the product is subpar and they're resting on their protected laurels.

Why Blind Buyers Are Wrong

The best product should be rewarded by being chosen. Period. Choosing based on any characteristic other than "best" is disrespectful to whoever is being favored because it implies they aren't capable of actually competing with the best. This is the capitalistic version of affirmative action, where businesses that are X-made or X-owned get by with subpar products simply because they have the X branding instead of actual skill.

I want to support local businesses! I want to support American businesses! I want to support marginalized groups! But they must deserve it. They have to compete for it for both themselves and the consumers. And the only way to help them compete is to buy the best and force others to improve.

Lies and Disrespect

Choosing a subpar product because of a characteristic feels borderline insulting and disrespectful, like indirectly lying to them about how good the product is and insinuating they can't do better; if you thought they could, all it would take is the nudge of not buying their product for them to get the message.

This again draws parallels to critics of affirmative action, where the critics wonder if undeserving recipients feel worse about getting selected because they weren't objectively as good as their less-AAed counterparts. The feelings probably go both ways. On one hand, it's a simple gloat of "I got into Harvard" and "I'm rich from my business" with an unspoken "because of my X status". On the other hand, it's a damage to the ego of those that want to be best because it simply means being the best and all that is associated with it (hard work, ingenuity, persistence).

My best guess is that the recipients don't care about how or why they reached their achievement, just that they did.

Limitations

There are obvious limitations to this:

  • Money matters. A small business with low funds will have difficulty outcompeting a large business with high funds. This is unfortunate and an arguably fair reason to choose the worse product.
