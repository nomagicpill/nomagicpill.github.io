Overview

Naltrexone is an opiod antagonist and "primarily used to manage alcohol use or opioid use disorder by reducing cravings and feelings of euphoria associated with substance use disorder". Over time, the user learns that the "bad activity" is no longer pleasurable and the addiction is reduced or eliminated altogether.

The evidence for alcohol and opioid is there, but what about other things, like eating, sex and porn, etc? Shouldn't it work for other pleasure-associated addictions?

I suspect yes, it can work for any pleasure-associated activity and I'm here to find out in the following categories:

Literature Review

The studies cited are not meant to be comprehensive or exhaustive; they are meant for me to familiarize myself with the research and have some quick references. Many more sources are available in the reference sections of each paper.

Pornography continues to be a struggle for many, especially men. Below are a list of studies supporting the use of naltrexone for dealing with sex-related addictions.

List of studies:

  • Kraus et al.'s Treatment of Compulsive Pornography Use With Naltrexone: A Case Report concludes:

    Our findings suggest that naltrexone may be a useful adjunctive treatment for patients reporting difficulty managing sexual behaviors, such as excessive viewing of Internet pornography. A notable limitation of the current case report is that we did not employ a reversal (A-B-A) design with Mr. D. Additionally, further testing in a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial evaluating the efficacy and tolerability of naltrexone with (or without) psychotherapy for the treatment of problematic pornography viewing and other compulsive sexual behaviors is needed.

  • Bostwick & Bucci's Internet Sex Addiction Treated With Naltrexone

    Adding naltrexone to a medication regimen that already included a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor coincided with a precipitous decline in and eventual resolution of his addictive symptoms, with a resultant renaissance of his social, occupational, and personal function. ... More research is needed to confirm that our observations can be generalized to other patients and to clarify the mechanism by which naltrexone extinguishes addictive behavior.

  • Raymond et al.'s Augmentation with naltrexone to treat compulsive sexual behavior: a case series found that:

    Seventeen (89%) of the 19 patients reported a reduction in CSB [compulsive sexual behavior] symptoms when taking naltrexone for a period ranging from 2 months to 2.3 years

  • Sultana & Din's Compulsive Sexual Behavior and Alcohol Use Disorder Treated With Naltrexone: A Case Report and Literature Review found:

    The patient reported that his “sexual compulsion” also reduced after the medication [naltrexone] and there was an improvement in both alcohol addiction and self-reported compulsive sexual behavior.

  • Grant & Kim's A case of kleptomania and compulsive sexual behavior treated with naltrexone found:

    Treatment with high-dose naltrexone (150 mg/day) led to remission of the patient's sexual urges

  • Ryback's Naltrexone in the treatment of adolescent sexual offenders found:

    Fifteen of 21 patients were considered to have a positive result and continued to respond for at least 4 months to an average dose of 160 mg per day with decreased sexual fantasies and masturbation. Dosages above 200 mg per day were not more helpful.
