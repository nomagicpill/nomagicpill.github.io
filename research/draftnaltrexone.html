Overview

Naltrexone is an opiod antagonist and "primarily used to manage alcohol use or opioid use disorder by reducing cravings and feelings of euphoria associated with substance use disorder". Over time, the user learns that the "bad activity" is no longer pleasurable and the addiction is reduced or eliminated altogether.

The evidence for alcohol is there, but what about other things, like eating, sex and porn, gambling etc? Shouldn't it work for other pleasure-associated addictions?

I suspect yes, it can work for any pleasure-associated activity and I'm here to find out in the following categories: food, nicotine, [FINISH]. These choices are either for my personal self-improvement, curiosity, and scouting out a potential, will-probably-never-happen business idea (the Sinclair Method, but for everything else).

There are philosophical implications to naltrexone and the Sinclair Method.

Experiments

There are a multitude of dimensions that can be tested across all the categories I chose, but alas, I'm time-, money-, and effort-limited, so I kept it simple and opted for a few. Each section includes background, pre-registered hypotheses, and results.

The design itself was relatively simple overall:

  1. Place X placebos and X naltrexones in an opaque container
  2. When I need to take one (depending on the type of test), put on gloves, blind myself, and take one with a swig of water
  3. Perform respective test
  4. Mark down results of that test
  5. Have partner determine which pill I took by counting the remaining pills or take a picture and review it at the end of the experiment

More specific design details for each test are in each subsection.

Some notes on my blinding protocol:

  • I have never experienced the effects of, tasted, or physically felt naltrexone. Regardless, I want to fend off any indicators by wearing gloves so as to not feel the texture, blindfolds so as not to see anything (my placebos are a different color), and drinking water to minimize any taste differences.
  • These are the placebo pills I used. 10 mm, white, microcrystalline cellulose. (Side note: A nice business idea would be to offer customizable placebo pills that match whatever you want. Simply give the business a link to your desired pill and get replicas in just a few days!)
  • I won't look at what I took on which day until the very end, else I may be able to tell certain feelings are associated with something.

Food

Background

I eat a lot because of my high-volume, medium-intensity bicycle and weightlifting training. Because sugar (carbs) are important to performance on the bike, I often use sugar—especially so-called "junk food", not just carb mixes—as fuel because it's a good excuse to eat it without guilt or negative health effects (up to a certain point). That said, my appetite has extended to off the bike and out of the gym when I don't need said "fuel", making it actual junk food at that point.

Specific Design

I chose three foods that I really like and mostly fit the fat-sugar profile that Claude recommended:

  • Pepperoni pizza, purchased from a local joint whose owner I've befriended
  • Ice cream sandwiches (specifically these)
  • Chocolate chip cookies (specifically these)

The overall protocol was as follows:

  1. Baseline: Nine (9) days total, trying each food three times on separate days. I eat a standard breakfast, fast for 8 hours, then eat said food and record data. Food choice is randomized, so it's not necessarily three repeats of pizza, ice cream, and cookies, in that order.
  2. Experimental: Same as the baseline days, but I take 50 mg naltrexone one hour before eating.

My data spreadsheet tracks the following info:

  • Enjoyment (1-10): Self-explanatory. How delicious was the food?
  • Pull/desire for more (1-10): Self-explanatory. How badly do I want more?
  • Sleep quality prior night (1-10): Measured by Fitbit sleep score (1-100).
  • Stress level (1-10): Measured subjectively.
  • Notes: Anything else I want to note.

The Philosophical Implications of Naltrexone Usage

Literature Review

The studies cited are not meant to be comprehensive or exhaustive; they are meant for me to familiarize myself with the research and have some quick references. Many more sources are available in the reference sections of each paper. I also had Claude Sonnet 4.6 do some research with the results here.

Pornography continues to be a struggle for many, especially men. Below are a list of studies supporting the use of naltrexone for dealing with sex-related addictions.

List of studies:

  • Kraus et al.'s Treatment of Compulsive Pornography Use With Naltrexone: A Case Report concludes:

    Our findings suggest that naltrexone may be a useful adjunctive treatment for patients reporting difficulty managing sexual behaviors, such as excessive viewing of Internet pornography. A notable limitation of the current case report is that we did not employ a reversal (A-B-A) design with Mr. D. Additionally, further testing in a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial evaluating the efficacy and tolerability of naltrexone with (or without) psychotherapy for the treatment of problematic pornography viewing and other compulsive sexual behaviors is needed.

  • Bostwick & Bucci's Internet Sex Addiction Treated With Naltrexone

    Adding naltrexone to a medication regimen that already included a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor coincided with a precipitous decline in and eventual resolution of his addictive symptoms, with a resultant renaissance of his social, occupational, and personal function. ... More research is needed to confirm that our observations can be generalized to other patients and to clarify the mechanism by which naltrexone extinguishes addictive behavior.

  • Raymond et al.'s Augmentation with naltrexone to treat compulsive sexual behavior: a case series found that:

    Seventeen (89%) of the 19 patients reported a reduction in CSB [compulsive sexual behavior] symptoms when taking naltrexone for a period ranging from 2 months to 2.3 years

  • Sultana & Din's Compulsive Sexual Behavior and Alcohol Use Disorder Treated With Naltrexone: A Case Report and Literature Review found:

    The patient reported that his “sexual compulsion” also reduced after the medication [naltrexone] and there was an improvement in both alcohol addiction and self-reported compulsive sexual behavior.

  • Grant & Kim's A case of kleptomania and compulsive sexual behavior treated with naltrexone found:

    Treatment with high-dose naltrexone (150 mg/day) led to remission of the patient's sexual urges

  • Ryback's Naltrexone in the treatment of adolescent sexual offenders found:

    Fifteen of 21 patients were considered to have a positive result and continued to respond for at least 4 months to an average dose of 160 mg per day with decreased sexual fantasies and masturbation. Dosages above 200 mg per day were not more helpful.

GLP-1s remain the weight loss king at the time of writing (November 2025) for scientific reasons I do not understand, but naltrexone may offer a similar mechanism in the food-is-less-appealing sense.

When consulting Claude on this, he suggested that "the opioid system is most implicated in sweet+fatty combinations", meaning there might be a weaker or stronger effect depending on the food.

List of studies:

  • Mason et al.'s Putting the brakes on the "drive to eat": Pilot effects of naltrexone and reward based eating on food cravings among obese women found:

    Opioidergic blockade did not reduce craving intensity; however, blockade reduced an association between trait-like reward-driven eating and daily food-craving intensity, and may help identify an important endophenotype within obesity.

  • Hicks et al.'s Use of naltrexone for treating binge eating and food obsession related to trauma: a case report and literature review found:

    naltrexone was prescribed and showed a marked reduction in these behaviors [binge eating, hoarding, and aggression related to food]

  • Valbrun & Zvonarev's The Opioid System and Food Intake: Use of Opiate Antagonists in Treatment of Binge Eating Disorder and Abnormal Eating Behavior has a nice literature review, finding that:

    the blockade of opioid receptors diminishes food intake. More recent findings also indicate that the combination of bupropion and naltrexone can induce weight loss.

List of studies:

  • Wart et al.'s The use of naltrexone in pathological and problem gambling: A UK case series found that:

    Patients showed significant decreases in their craving to gamble and the majority (60%) were able to abstain completely from gambling in the treatment period, with a further 20% reducing their gambling to almost nothing.
