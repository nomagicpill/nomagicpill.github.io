Taiwan

Taiwan trip report from 23-30 November 2025. This is more a collection of thoughts and observations about Taiwan than a play-by-play report.

Food

raohe night market, part of the allure of night markets is to go when it's poppin and ALL the vendors are open, we got pepper buns, mochi balls, seaweed fries, watermelon juice, okinomiyaki no fat people

The food here is as amazing as it is plentiful as it is cheap. Restaurants line every street and alleyway; some are crowded with lines and others are empty with bored shopowners, but both have plenty of food at the ready for hungry customers. Nor are the restaurants the same. Sure, beef noodles and buns remain some of the staple dishes, but plenty of other options exist, from turnip cakes (lo bak go) to Chinese omelettes to Taiwanese burgers (gua bao) to steamed buns to raw eel. It's also difficult to state the quality (in terms of tastiness, not quality-quality) of food here.

And cheap! Taiwanese food is probably 3-5x cheaper than the equivalent American meal. What is this economic sorcery you ask? [FINISH; WHY IS FOOD SO CHEAP; TALK ABOUT HOW TRAVELING TO AMERICA WOULD BE INSANELY EXPENSIVE FOR THEM]

And healthy(-ish)! [HEALTHY]

Taiwanese people eat out for most meals since comparative advantage is actually a real thing. The food is cheaper because the restaurants and vendors buy in bulk and it's more delicious because, well, they're chefs and have practiced their craft for quite some time. In multi-generation homes (i.e., children, parents, and grandparents all live together), the grandparents will often get groceries from the local street markets during the day and have food ready for everyone when they get home.

All of this makes Taiwan a food lover's paradise. Cheap, unique, delicious food is everywhere, served up by friendly people.

Fun Food

Taiwan is a land of superstition and it shows in two food-related ways:

  • Pineapple:
    In Taiwanese the pronunciation of the word pineapple sounds like a propitious blessing of good fortune and future prosperity. ... The pineapple has been referred to in traditional culture as the best gift for a house warming party and upon the opening of a new business or to wish one’s favorite political candidate success at the election boxes.
    Bittersweetly, lawyers, nurses, and police officers are exempt from this tasty tradition because their job prosperity means crime and sickness. [ADD IMAGE OF TAIWANESE POLICE OFFICER EATING PINEAPPLE]
  • Kuai Kuai culture:
    A phenomenon in Taiwan wherein people put snacks of the brand Kuai Kuai next to or on top of machines. People who do this believe that, because the name of the snack—"Kuai Kuai"—stands for "obedient" or "well-behaved," it will make a device function without errors.
    Apparently TSMC are big proponents of this. Maybe this is their secret sauce, not their insane work culture!

Like everywhere with any business, the places that had massive lines or were filled up with customers were often the most delicious, the most TikToked, or some combination of both. One place had almost 80 people in line!

Cleanliness

Taiwanese trash trucks collect in afternoon and evening; purpose of no trash cans is to remind you of how much waste you produce, prevent stuff from festering on the streets, encourage proper recycling; https://99percentinvisible.org/episode/separation-anxiety/

Walking around any streets you'd think that Taiwan had invented the 2 nm transistor node before trash cans. They are few and far in between for a few apparent reasons:

  • Having to hold your trash makes you a bit more conscious about your consumption
  • Having to hold your trash until you get home encourages proper sorting of trash vs. recycling. Taiwan's recycling rate is an astounding 55%, compared to the U.S.'s 32%.
  • Fewer trash cans means less smell and fewer overflowing cans per unit area

There's an unspoken rule about being able to pop into any of the numerous 7/11s to dispose of small items. We came up with the system of using a small bag to hold our trash while we walked around.

Litter was almost non-existent, even in the extremely crowded night markets where it's easy to drop stuff and difficult to pick back up. There was even a human street sweeper outside our hotel sweeping leaves into a dust bin.

Transportation

Scooters reign supreme. Parking is easier to find and actually perform; the low(er) speeds of cars are less likely to kill you in a collision; infrastructure is made for you (there is a scooter-only section in front of cars at stop lights). I don't think I've ever seen so many scooters in one place.

All vehicles are super courteous to pedestrians, although I did almost get run over once by a driver who was probably on their phone.
